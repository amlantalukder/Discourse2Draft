# CRISPR-based therapeutic genome editing for inherited blood disorders
**Abstract**

Therapeutic genome editing promises to transform medicine. Pivotal discoveries have provided a diverse and versatile set of tools to correct pathogenic mutations or produce protective alleles using CRISPR-based technologies. These innovative therapies are especially adaptable for blood and immune disorders, where clinical methods allow haematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) to be mobilized, harvested, engineered ex vivo and transplanted back into a patient to permanently replace their blood system. This paradigm has been exemplified with the first US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved CRISPR–Cas9 therapy for sickle cell disease and β-thalassaemia, exa-cel (Casgevy). Although promising, efficient delivery of gene edits involves complicated ex vivo manipulation and toxic myeloablative conditioning. The quiescent and elusive nature of HSCs also brings associated challenges. In this Review, we explore the state-of-the-art genome editing technologies of nucleases, base editors and prime editors, which hold promise to address unmet clinical needs for patients with inherited haematological disorders. We highlight the progress made for several disorders and discuss the challenges that remain for ex vivo and in vivo targeting of HSCs for next-generation gene therapies.
## Introduction
CRISPR-based therapeutic genome editing has emerged as a transformative approach in addressing inherited blood disorders by enabling precise, targeted modifications of the genome in hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) (CS = 10). In this context, the CRISPR–Cas9 system, guided by a short RNA sequence, introduces specific double-strand breaks at the targeted locus, thereby initiating endogenous repair processes such as non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) and homology-directed repair (HDR) (CS = 10). Moreover, the recent development of base and prime editors has advanced this platform further by allowing for the conversion of single nucleotides and the introduction of small insertions or deletions without the need for double-strand breaks, thereby minimizing collateral damage to the genomic integrity (CS = 9) [1].

The clinical translation of CRISPR-based genome editing for blood disorders, such as sickle cell disease and β-thalassemia, is underpinned by rigorous insights into HSC biology and the challenges inherent in ex vivo manipulations (CS = 9). Critical to this progress is the ability to harvest, engineer, and subsequently reinfuse modified HSCs to achieve long-term, curative outcomes. However, the quiescent and delicate nature of HSCs necessitates sophisticated approaches to both delivery and gene modification that guard against potential off-target effects, adverse immune responses, and the toxicity associated with myeloablative conditioning (CS = 8). Ongoing advances in the expansion, identification, and culturing of HSCs, combined with continuous improvements in the specificity and efficiency of CRISPR technologies, hold significant promise in overcoming current limitations and propel these therapies into routine clinical application (CS = 8) [2, 3].
## Genome editing technologies
Genome editing technologies have emerged as a cornerstone in the development of gene therapies for inherited blood disorders, providing the means to introduce targeted modifications in the genome with unparalleled precision (CS=10). Foundational platforms such as zinc-finger nucleases (ZFNs) and transcription activator-like effector nucleases (TALENs) rely on engineered protein-DNA interactions to create double-strand breaks (DSBs) at predetermined loci; however, the RNA-guided CRISPR-Cas systems have rapidly supplanted these earlier methods due to their ease of design, scalability, and cost-effectiveness (CS=10) [1, 4].

The induction of DSBs by these programmable nucleases activates endogenous cellular repair pathways, predominantly non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) and homology-directed repair (HDR), which respectively enable the creation of gene knockouts or the precise incorporation of corrective sequences when provided with a donor template (CS=10). Recent innovations within the CRISPR-based toolkit have given rise to base editors and prime editors. Base editing systems, which combine deaminase enzymes with a catalytically impaired or nickase version of Cas proteins, facilitate the direct conversion of one nucleotide to another without the formation of DSBs, thereby reducing the potential for unwanted genomic rearrangements (CS=9). Prime editing, in turn, builds upon this approach by fusing a reverse transcriptase domain to an engineered Cas protein, thereby expanding the repertoire of possible modifications to include substitutions, small insertions, and deletions with improved accuracy and minimized genomic disruption (CS=9) [1, 4].

Together, these evolving genome editing modalities underscore a paradigm shift in therapeutic genome engineering. Their precise and multifaceted capabilities not only facilitate the correction of pathogenic mutations in disorders such as sickle cell disease and β-thalassemia, but also support the generation of protective genetic variants to ameliorate disease phenotypes (CS=10). Importantly, the transition from early programmable nucleases to next-generation base and prime editors reflects a concerted effort to mitigate off-target effects and enhance safety profiles, ultimately paving the way for more reliable and effective clinical applications in hematopoietic stem cell manipulation (CS=10) [1, 4].
### CRISPR–Cas nucleases
CRISPR–Cas nucleases have rapidly become the cornerstone of contemporary genome editing, especially in therapeutic contexts aimed at correcting the genetic defects underlying inherited blood disorders (CS=10) [1, 4]. In contrast to earlier programmable nucleases that relied on complex protein–DNA interactions, the CRISPR–Cas9 system harnesses an RNA-guided mechanism for target recognition. This system employs a single guide RNA (sgRNA) that confers specificity through Watson–Crick base pairing with a 20‐nucleotide sequence in the target DNA, thereby directing the Cas9 nuclease to its cleavage site adjacent to a short protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) (CS=10) [1, 5].

At the molecular level, the Cas9 enzyme exhibits two catalytic domains: HNH, which cleaves the DNA strand complementary to the guide RNA, and RuvC, which cleaves the non‐target strand. This dual-nuclease configuration results in the generation of a double-strand break (DSB) at a defined position relative to the PAM, thereby initiating endogenous DNA repair pathways such as non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) and homology-directed repair (HDR) (CS=10) [1, 6]. The operational simplicity and flexibility of the CRISPR–Cas9 complex, in comparison to zinc-finger nucleases and TALENs, not only enhances its efficacy but also reduces the time and cost associated with reprogramming the system for different genomic targets (CS=10) [1, 4].

Recent developments have extended the capabilities of CRISPR–Cas nucleases beyond conventional DSB formation. Innovations such as base editing and prime editing allow for precise nucleotide conversions or small insertions/deletions without resorting to DSBs, thereby minimizing the risks of undesired genomic rearrangements and off-target effects (CS=9) [1, 4]. Moreover, ongoing structural and mechanistic studies continue to refine our understanding of the conformational dynamics involved in target DNA recognition and cleavage, which in turn informs the development of Cas variants with enhanced specificity and broader PAM compatibility. These enhancements are critical for advancing the therapeutic application of CRISPR-based genome editing in hematological diseases (CS=8) [1, 5].
### Base editors
Base editors represent a significant evolution in genome editing that circumvents the need for double-strand breaks (DSBs) by directly converting one nucleotide to another, thereby enabling precise modifications of single base pairs without eliciting the error-prone mechanisms associated with conventional nuclease-induced repair (CS=10). In these systems, a catalytically impaired Cas9—typically in the form of a nickase—is fused to a single-stranded DNA-modifying enzyme, such as a cytidine deaminase or an adenine deaminase. Upon recognition of the target sequence via the guide RNA (gRNA), the Cas9 component facilitates binding and formation of an R-loop, which exposes a single-stranded region of the DNA. This exposed strand is then subject to enzymatic deamination; in the case of cytosine base editors (CBEs), cytidine deaminase converts cytosine to uracil, which is subsequently interpreted as thymine during DNA replication, resulting in a C•G to T•A transition (CS=10). The inclusion of an optional uracil glycosylase inhibitor (UGI) further enhances the editing efficiency by preventing the cellular base excision repair machinery from reversing the conversion (CS=9). Similarly, adenine base editors (ABEs) exploit an analogous mechanism whereby adenine is deaminated to inosine, which is read as guanine, thereby enabling an A•T to G•C transition (CS=9). 

The precision and reduced genotoxicity of base editors position them as promising candidates for therapeutic interventions in inherited blood disorders such as sickle cell disease and β-thalassemia, where the correction of single-point mutations in hematopoietic stem cells can have profound clinical implications (CS=10). Despite challenges, including constraints imposed by protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) requirements and a relatively narrow editing window, ongoing refinements in enzyme engineering and delivery strategies continue to enhance both efficiency and specificity. These improvements are expected to further reduce off-target effects, making base editors a robust tool not only in preclinical models but also in prospective clinical applications for treating genetic hematologic disorders (CS=10) [1].
### Prime editors
Prime editors represent a significant advancement over conventional CRISPR-Cas9 systems by enabling a wide spectrum of precise genomic alterations—including all twelve possible point mutations, small insertions, and deletions—without the need to create deleterious double-strand breaks (DSBs) (CS=9) [7]. This technology hinges on the fusion of a Cas9 nickase, which retains its ability to search for target DNA but makes only a single-stranded break, with an engineered reverse transcriptase. The process is directed by a prime editing guide RNA (pegRNA) that not only specifies the target site but also encodes the desired nucleic acid change through a reverse transcriptase template coupled with a primer-binding site. Together, these components mediate the reverse transcription of the intended edit directly into the target locus, thereby circumventing the reliance on error-prone repair pathways such as non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) (CS=9) [7].

Recent developments have further expanded the utility of prime editors beyond mammalian cells to include plant systems and even ex vivo applications in hematopoietic stem cells. In these contexts, modifications to both the prime editor protein architecture and the pegRNA design have been critical. For instance, strategies such as split prime editing—where the Cas9 nickase and the reverse transcriptase are expressed as separate polypeptides—and the incorporation of optimized 3ʹ extensions in the pegRNA scaffold have demonstrably increased editing efficiencies and fidelity. These enhancements serve to reduce bystander mutations and minimize off-target effects, thereby elevating the safety profile of prime editors and underscoring their potential in therapeutic gene correction, especially for inherited blood disorders (CS=8) [7].

Although prime editing is still in an emerging phase relative to other genome editing modalities, its inherent versatility and precision position it as a promising tool for clinical applications. Its capacity to introduce virtually any single-nucleotide alteration, along with small insertions and deletions, without resorting to DSB-induced repair, marks a paradigm shift in gene editing. Ongoing optimization in delivery strategies and the fine-tuning of prime editor components are expected to further enhance its clinical viability, particularly for ex vivo manipulation of hematopoietic stem cells where genomic safety and efficiency are paramount (CS=8) [1, 7].
### Emerging CRISPR-based platforms for targeted transgene integration
Recent advancements in CRISPR‐based platforms have led to the development of innovative strategies for targeted transgene integration, addressing historical issues of low efficiency and undesirable random insertion events (CS=9). Researchers have implemented multiple approaches to enhance the precision of donor integration by redesigning the transgene donor template to include homologous arms flanking the desired sequence and incorporating regulatory elements such as splice acceptor cassettes that only activate expression upon correct, on‐target integration. Moreover, modulation of the cellular DNA repair machinery—such as the augmentation of homology‐directed repair or the regulation of Cas9 activity via cell cycle synchronization—has been demonstrated to favor targeted insertion over error-prone pathways. These methodological improvements, including the use of matched end joining designs where the donor template is pre‐flanked by sgRNA target sites, help ensure that only correctly integrated transgenes yield detectable reporter signals, thereby minimizing the occurrence of off-target integration events (CS=9) [8].

In parallel, modular strategies employing safe harbor loci have been established to enable reproducible and predictable expression of therapeutic transgenes, which is particularly critical for the treatment of inherited blood disorders via hematopoietic stem cell modification (CS=8). By selecting genomic loci that are transcriptionally active yet insulated from disrupting endogenous gene function, investigators guarantee long-term, stable transgene expression with minimal insertional mutagenesis. Complementary approaches have also explored non-viral delivery techniques, which help to transiently express the CRISPR machinery and reduce unwanted genomic alterations, and the use of split selection markers to further refine the identification of correctly targeted cells. Together, these combined strategies—ranging from the design of sophisticated donor templates to the careful selection of safe harbor integration sites—illustrate the transformative potential of emerging CRISPR-based systems in executing precise, efficient, and clinically relevant transgene integrations (CS=8) [9].
## Therapeutic opportunities for haematological disorders

### β-Haemoglobinopathies
The advent of CRISPR‐based genome editing has ushered in unprecedented therapeutic opportunities for β‐haemoglobinopathies, including sickle cell disease (SCD) and β‐thalassemia. These disorders are fundamentally linked to specific point mutations in the β‐globin gene that affect the structure or expression of the β‐globin chain. For example, in SCD, a single nucleotide substitution converts a glutamic acid residue to valine at the sixth position, thereby promoting hemoglobin polymerization and resulting in the characteristic sickle shape of red blood cells (CS=10) [1]. In β‐thalassemia, mutations lead to decreased or absent synthesis of β‐globin chains, which in turn disrupts the stoichiometry of globin production, leading to ineffective erythropoiesis and severe anemia (CS=10) [1].

Advances in genome editing have introduced various strategies to correct these mutations. Traditional CRISPR–Cas9 systems rely on the induction of double‐strand breaks and subsequent repair via non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) or homology-directed repair (HDR), but these methods can introduce undesired insertions or deletions (indels) (CS=9). To overcome these limitations, base editors have been developed that fuse a catalytically impaired Cas9 with enzymes such as cytidine deaminases to mediate precise C•G to T•A conversions without generating double-strand breaks (CS=9) [10]. More recently, prime editing has expanded the editing repertoire by combining a Cas9 nickase with a reverse transcriptase and a specialized prime editing guide RNA (pegRNA), thereby facilitating a full spectrum of base substitutions, small insertions, or deletions with enhanced precision (CS=8) [7].

Delivery of these sophisticated editing reagents to hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), which are characteristically quiescent in vivo, remains one of the major challenges to clinical translation. A range of strategies—including optimization of vector systems and ex vivo culture protocols—aims to enhance the efficiency with which CRISPR components are introduced into HSCs while preserving their long-term repopulating potential (CS=9) [2]. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that gene correction in HSCs via these refined CRISPR-based methods can restore the expression of functional β-globin, promising a durable cure with minimal off-target genomic perturbations (CS=8) [11].

Clinical translational efforts have already begun to validate these approaches, with early trials reporting substantial therapeutic efficacy. The precision achieved by both base and prime editors, combined with ongoing improvements in delivery methods and off-target minimization, underscores their potential to provide personalized, mutation-specific treatments for patients with SCD and β-thalassemia (CS=9) [12]. Overall, the integration of next-generation editing tools into ex vivo manipulation protocols for HSCs represents a paradigm shift in the treatment of inherited blood disorders, offering a promising avenue toward sustainable, curative therapies (CS=9) [1, 10].
### Primary immunodeficiencies
Primary immunodeficiencies comprise a heterogeneous group of inherited disorders in which components of the immune system are deficient or dysfunctional, leading to life‐threatening infections and immune dysregulation (CS = 10). Among these conditions, severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) and deficiencies of recombination activating genes (RAG1/RAG2) represent paradigmatic examples where defects in lymphocyte development and V(D)J recombination underlie the immunopathology. CRISPR–Cas9 genome editing, by virtue of its RNA‐guided target recognition and its ability to induce site‐specific DNA double‐strand breaks or precise, nick-mediated modifications, has emerged as a powerful therapeutic modality for directly correcting these pathogenic mutations in hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) (CS = 10) [13].

In preclinical models, the application of CRISPR-based strategies has demonstrated promising outcomes in reconstituting functional immune cells. For instance, correction of RAG deficiencies using CRISPR–Cas9 in HSPCs not only restores the molecular integrity of the RAG genes but also enables the recapitulation of normal V(D)J recombination processes essential for diverse antigen receptor repertoires in T and B lymphocytes (CS = 9). This approach relies on ex vivo modification of autologous HSPCs, wherein engineered cells, once verified for on-target correction and minimal off-target effects, are reinfused into patients. Such strategies, which may incorporate either homology-directed repair with donor templates or the emerging base and prime editing modalities that bypass the need for double-strand break induction, hold significant promise to correct not only loss-of-function mutations but also to modulate aberrant gene expression profiles underlying complex immune dysregulation (CS = 9) [13].

Despite these encouraging developments, several technical and translational challenges remain. Optimization of editing efficiency, precise donor delivery, and minimization of off-target events, particularly in quiescent HSCs, are critical for ensuring long-term engraftment and robust immune reconstitution. In addition, the inherent risks associated with insertional mutagenesis and the maintenance of genomic integrity require rigorous preclinical assessment and the development of high-fidelity nucleases. Emerging CRISPR modalities, including next-generation base and prime editing platforms, offer alternative approaches that may further reduce genotoxicity while enabling the correction of a broader spectrum of point mutations and small indels. Collectively, these innovations have significant clinical potential for the treatment of primary immunodeficiencies, promising durable restoration of immune function with manageable safety profiles (CS = 9) [13].
### Bone marrow failure syndromes
Bone marrow failure syndromes represent a clinically and genetically heterogeneous group of inherited disorders characterized by impaired production of one or more blood cell lineages, frequently predisposing affected individuals to leukemia and other complications (CS=10). Classic examples include Fanconi anemia, Diamond–Blackfan anemia, and Shwachman–Diamond syndrome, each linked to disruptions in key cellular pathways such as DNA repair, ribosome biogenesis, and telomere maintenance (CS=10) [14]. Advances in CRISPR-based therapeutic genome editing provide a promising approach to directly correct the underlying pathogenic mutations in hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells. By precisely targeting defective alleles and potentially restoring normal gene function, CRISPR modalities offer the opportunity to re-establish effective hematopoiesis and ameliorate the cytopenias observed in these syndromes (CS=9) [14].

Recent experimental studies, including those using zebrafish models, have underscored the critical role of TP53 pathway activation in the pathophysiology of bone marrow failure. Aberrant TP53 responses, triggered by cellular stress from ribosomal defects or telomere dysfunction, contribute to increased apoptosis and defective erythropoiesis in these patients (CS=9). CRISPR-based strategies that facilitate precise genomic modifications—such as base and prime editing technologies—could reduce inadvertent activation of these stress pathways by avoiding double-strand breaks, thereby potentially mitigating excessive TP53-dependent cell death while preserving the proliferative capacity of hematopoietic progenitors (CS=9) [14].

In addition to genetic correction, the success of these therapies critically depends on the efficient delivery of CRISPR components to quiescent hematopoietic stem cells, a process that is complicated by the unique characteristics of the bone marrow niche and the need to maintain stemness during ex vivo manipulation (CS=9). Strategies such as optimized ex vivo expansion, targeted integration into safe harbor genomic loci, and refined donor template design are essential to maximize engraftment and durability of the corrected HSCs (CS=9). By employing high-fidelity Cas enzymes and innovative delivery systems, recent developments aim not only to enhance the precision of genome editing but also to minimize off-target modifications and unwanted genomic alterations, thereby reducing the risk of secondary complications (CS=9) [14].
## Therapeutic challenges

### Genotoxic risks
CRISPR‐based genome editing, by virtue of generating targeted double‐strand breaks (DSBs) in DNA, inherently poses significant genotoxic risks that must be carefully weighed in therapeutic applications for inherited blood disorders (CS = 10). The predominant repair mechanism for these DSBs, non‐homologous end joining (NHEJ), is error‐prone and frequently results in small insertions or deletions (indels) at the cleavage site, potentially disrupting gene function not only at the target locus but also at off‐target sites where sequence mismatches may be tolerated (CS = 10). Furthermore, inadvertent chromosomal rearrangements and complex genomic aberrations have been observed in preclinical models when DSBs exceed the cell’s capacity for faithful repair, thereby raising concerns over long-term genomic stability and potential oncogenic transformation, especially in hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) that are intended for therapeutic transplantation (CS = 9) [1].

Recent advances in CRISPR technologies, including the development of high-fidelity Cas9 variants and DSB‐independent editing tools such as base and prime editors, have aimed to mitigate these genotoxic challenges by minimizing the formation of DSBs and, consequently, reducing the incidence of indels and off‐target modifications (CS = 9). Nevertheless, even these next-generation tools carry risks—for instance, base editors can induce bystander nucleotide alterations within the deamination window, whereas prime editing, although more versatile and precise, may still lead to unintended modifications if the reverse transcriptase activity or the pegRNA design is suboptimal (CS = 8). Given that the long-term persistence of edited HSCs is critical for durable therapeutic benefit, extensive preclinical screening employing unbiased next-generation sequencing techniques is indispensable for detecting and quantifying any off-target or on-target undesired events before clinical translation (CS = 9) [15].
### Targeting quiescent HSCs
Targeting quiescent hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) for therapeutic genome editing presents unique challenges due to their intrinsic state of deep dormancy combined with stringent cell cycle control. Quiescent HSCs, particularly long-term repopulating cells, maintain their regenerative capacity through a tightly regulated balance of cyclin-dependent kinases, cyclins, and CDK inhibitors, and are characterized by reduced metabolic activity and minimal cellular division events (CS=9). This state, maintained in part by signaling pathways such as TGFβ and mechanisms regulating lysosomal activity, minimizes the uptake of CRISPR components and hampers the activation of endogenous DNA repair processes required for precise gene editing (CS=8) [16, 17].

Further complicating therapeutic intervention, the physical and biochemical barriers inherent to non-dividing cells necessitate advanced and targeted delivery systems. Nanoparticle-based non-viral vectors, tailored for stimulus-responsive release, offer a promising strategy to overcome electrostatic repulsion and ensure spatially and temporally regulated CRISPR-Cas9 delivery; however, their efficiency remains lower relative to viral vectors (CS=7). In parallel, the preference of quiescent cells for non-homologous end joining over homology-directed repair further diminishes the efficiency of precision editing strategies. As a result, innovative approaches such as transient HSC activation, or the application of next-generation editors like base and prime editors—which bypass the need for double-strand break induction—are under active investigation to enhance gene editing outcomes while preserving stem cell functionality (CS=8) [5, 18].
Transient activation strategies have emerged as a promising approach to overcome the intrinsic quiescence of hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) for effective CRISPR-mediated genome editing. Given that long-term repopulating HSCs are maintained in a deeply dormant state, transiently activating these cells can promote entry into the cell cycle, thereby enhancing the cellular uptake of CRISPR components and improving the efficiency of DNA repair mechanisms, such as homology-directed repair, during the S/G2 phase (CS=9). This approach involves the use of precise pharmacological or cytokine-mediated stimuli that temporarily shift HSCs from a quiescent, deeply dormant state into a more readily editable, short-term progenitor-like state while minimizing the risk of compromising their stemness and long-term regenerative potential (CS=9) [17].

The transient activation process must be carefully calibrated, as overstimulation or prolonged cell cycle entry may inadvertently deplete the stem cell pool or lead to premature differentiation, thereby undermining the therapeutic goal of sustained hematopoiesis (CS=9). Recent studies have outlined optimized ex vivo culture conditions that maintain HSC viability and ‘stemness’ even during transient activation, ensuring that edited cells retain their capacity for long-term engraftment upon autologous transplantation (CS=8). Furthermore, integrating transient activation with next-generation genome editors, such as base and prime editors that obviate the requirement for double-strand break induction, provides a dual-faceted strategy; this allows for genomic correction with minimized genotoxic stress while preserving the intrinsic self-renewal characteristics of the HSCs (CS=9) [19].

### Chemotherapy-free cell therapies
The bone marrow failure syndromes encompass a heterogeneous group of rare, monogenic disorders that lead to reduced hematopoietic output due to defects in diverse cellular processes such as DNA damage response, telomere maintenance, ribosome biogenesis, or proteostasis. In patients with β‐haemoglobinopathies, the molecular pathology is equally complex. In contrast, β‐thalassemia typically involves mutations that cause reduced or absent production of beta-globin chains, with consequent accumulation of unpaired alpha-globin precipitates in erythroid precursors (CS=10). Together, these disorders contribute significantly to global morbidity, particularly in populations from the Mediterranean, Asia, and Africa (CS=10) [1].

The efficiency of these approaches relies on the delicate balance between error-prone non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) and high-fidelity homology-directed repair (HDR) pathways (CS=9). While HDR has enabled precise correction by templated repair, its restricted activity during the S/G2 cell cycle phases limits its use in quiescent HSCs (CS=9). In order to overcome these limitations, alternative techniques such as base editing and prime editing have been developed; the former allows single nucleotide modifications without double-strand break (DSB) formation, and the latter further enables targeted insertions or deletions while avoiding DSBs (CS=9). Moreover, targeted integration strategies that use safe harbor loci further mitigate the risks associated with random integration, thereby enhancing the precision and safety of gene correction in autologous HSC therapy (CS=9) [8, 11].

It is also critical to consider that the quiescent state of HSCs, maintained by a unique metabolic and transcriptional profile in a hypoxic bone marrow niche, poses additional challenges for efficient genome editing. Quiescence minimizes replication-induced errors but also restricts access to HDR-mediated repair pathways (CS=9). Recent advances in our understanding of stem cell quiescence have highlighted the role of key regulators—including HIF-1α and Meis1—in balancing glycolytic flux and mitochondrial respiration, thereby preserving the long-term regenerative potential of HSCs (CS=9) [17, 22].

Collectively, these developments underscore both the potential and the challenges of applying CRISPR-based genome editing to treat inherited blood disorders. ### In vivo delivery
In vivo delivery remains a critical challenge for broad patient access to CRISPR‐based therapeutic genome editing for inherited blood disorders (CS = 10). The success of in vivo strategies depends on overcoming the biological barriers that limit the stability, targeting, and cellular uptake of CRISPR components. For instance, following systemic administration, the Cas9 protein and its guide RNA must evade rapid degradation, immune clearance, and nonspecific uptake by non‐target tissues; in parallel, they must traverse the vascular endothelium and efficiently enter the desired cell type (CS = 10) [5, 15].

Non‐viral delivery systems, such as lipid nanoparticles, polymeric nanocarriers, and extracellular vesicles, have emerged as promising alternatives to traditional viral vectors. Their stimulus-responsive designs allow for controlled release of the CRISPR components in response to specific physiological cues—such as changes in pH or redox state—thus enhancing spatial and temporal control of gene editing (CS = 9). Additionally, these nanoparticles can be engineered to display targeting ligands that recognize markers on diseased cells, further improving cell-specific delivery and reducing off-target effects (CS = 9) [5, 23].

Another key consideration in in vivo delivery is the challenge of transporting CRISPR reagents to less accessible cell populations, such as quiescent hematopoietic stem cells residing in specialized bone marrow niches. Quiescent cells display low rates of endocytosis and robust barriers to intracellular trafficking, which necessitates the development of vehicles that not only enhance cellular uptake but also promote efficient endosomal escape and nuclear entry (CS = 8). Strategies that incorporate stimuli-responsive features—designed to release their payload upon encountering endosomal pH or enzymatic activity—have begun to address these limitations, thereby increasing the likelihood of achieving therapeutic editing levels (CS = 8) [17, 22].

Furthermore, the formulation of CRISPR delivery platforms must be optimized to balance safety, efficacy, and manufacturing scalability. Non-viral nanoparticle systems offer advantages in terms of lower immunogenicity and easier scale-up compared to viral vectors, yet their efficiency still lags behind that of viruses. Recent advances in nanotechnology have enabled the design of multifunctional carriers that improve loading capacity, protect the cargo from nuclease degradation, and provide controlled release, thereby enhancing the overall gene editing efficiency in vivo (CS = 8) [5, 15].

Collectively, these advances in stimuli-responsive nanocarriers and targeted delivery strategies underscore the potential of in vivo CRISPR delivery systems to expand patient access to gene editing therapies. By mitigating key challenges—such as immune clearance, limited cellular uptake, and off-target effects—these innovative approaches are paving the way for more effective, clinically translatable treatments for inherited blood disorders (CS = 9) [13, 23].

Taken together, these developments in process automation and smart manufacturing are converging to create simplified manufacturing workflows that are both cost-effective and robust. By seamlessly integrating continuous improvements in upstream cell culture, bioreactor design, and downstream processing with precisely engineered non-viral delivery systems and cost-effective scaling strategies, the field is poised to overcome previous limitations in bioindustrial production. The use of standardized manufacturing readiness frameworks and automated quality control will be essential for ensuring that therapeutic cells can be produced reliably at a scale that can broaden patient access to these transformative CRISPR-based treatments (CS=10) [5].

Building on the pioneering work with CRISPR‐Cas9—which introduced double‐strand breaks (CS=10)—the field has seen a paradigm shift with the advent of base and prime editors that enable single nucleotide modifications and small indels with minimized collateral genome damage (CS=9). Concurrent improvements in the fidelity of Cas nucleases, achieved through engineering variants with reduced off-target activity, have contributed to a more favorable safety profile. These refinements are complemented by robust off-target evaluation methods, including predictive modeling and deep sequencing, which together form a critical framework for assessing editing specificity in sensitive cell populations (CS=9) [11, 13].

Looking forward, the integration of advanced non-viral delivery systems, such as stimuli-responsive nanoparticles and refined extracellular vehicles, is addressing the longstanding challenges related to targeted delivery, immune clearance, and manufacturing scalability (CS=8). The convergence of automation in cell processing, coupled with innovative regulatory and manufacturing strategies, is poised to accelerate the translation of these therapies into clinical practice while ensuring consistent product quality (CS=8). In addition, multidisciplinary efforts aimed at optimizing both in vivo and ex vivo approaches will be essential to harness the full therapeutic potential of CRISPR-based interventions. Overall, while significant challenges remain in ensuring long-term safety and efficient tissue-specific delivery, the cumulative progress in the field reinforces a future where precise, curative therapies for inherited blood disorders become broadly accessible (CS=8) [5, 12, 15].
