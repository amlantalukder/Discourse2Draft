# Title: Phthalate Toxicity: Mechanistic Insights, Exposure Assessment, Epidemiology, and Regulatory Perspectives
## Executive Summary
Current research on phthalates needs to address long-term, low-level exposures and their effects passed to future generations. Studies should use real-world mixtures and consistent laboratory and animal testing methods to understand how different chemicals interact and accumulate in the body. Collaboration among epidemiologists, molecular scientists, and public health experts is essential to collect comprehensive data and identify early signs of harm in diverse communities. Regular updates to monitoring practices will help track new chemical substitutes and combined exposures. On the policy front, regulations must consider the effects of chemical mixtures and protect vulnerable groups through precautionary measures. Incorporating evidence of inherited changes will support earlier interventions. Coordinated efforts among researchers, regulators, and industry are crucial to translate scientific findings into policies that prevent harmful exposures and safeguard public health.

## Classification and Exposure Assessment
Phthalates are commonly classified based on their alkyl chain length into low–molecular-weight (LMW) phthalates (e.g., dimethyl phthalate [DMP] and diethyl phthalate [DEP]) and high–molecular-weight (HMW) phthalates (e.g., di(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate [DEHP] and diisononyl phthalate [DiNP])  [1, 2]. LMW phthalates generally feature shorter carbon backbones (C1–C6), while HMW phthalates exhibit longer chains (C7–C13)  [2]. Widely used in plastic production, DMP is frequently encountered as it confers flexibility and durability to polymers  [3], whereas DEHP and DiNP are among the most prevalent HMW phthalates in polyvinyl chloride (PVC) formulations  [4].

Because they are not chemically bonded to the polymer matrix, phthalates readily migrate from products into the environment, leading to exposure through ingestion, inhalation, dermal contact, and parenteral routes  [5]. Personal-care items, including fragrances and cosmetics, represent a significant source of LMW phthalate exposure, with DMP and DEP commonly used to prolong the longevity of scents  [6]. Additionally, HMW phthalates in PVC-based medical devices can leach into intravenous solutions, raising concerns regarding parenteral exposure  [6]. Environmental contamination is further evidenced by their presence in dust, soil, and water samples worldwide  [1].
### A. Low–Molecular-Weight (LMW) Phthalates
Low–molecular-weight (LMW) phthalates, characterized by carbon backbones generally spanning C1–C6, are commonly applied in personal care products, such as fragrances and cosmetics, where compounds like dimethyl phthalate (DMP) and diethyl phthalate (DEP) serve as solvents and fixatives to prolong scent longevity  [2, 6]. DMP in particular can be found in a broad range of applications, including insect repellents and flexible plastic products, although it is known to exhibit environmental persistence with an estimated half-life of up to 20 years under certain conditions  [3, 7]. Because LMW phthalates are not bonded covalently to polymer matrices, they leach easily into surrounding media, contributing to exposure through dermal contact, inhalation, or ingestion  [5]. Furthermore, sensitive populations may be disproportionately affected, given that LMW phthalates readily volatilize and can be absorbed through the skin from personal care products. Numerous biomonitoring studies thus measure their urinary metabolites to evaluate individual phthalate burdens and assess potential health risks associated with their endocrine-disrupting properties  [1].
### B. High–Molecular-Weight (HMW) Phthalates
High–molecular-weight (HMW) phthalates generally comprise compounds with C7–C13 carbon backbones in their alkyl side chains, a structural feature that differentiates them from their low–molecular-weight counterparts and confers distinct physicochemical and toxicological profiles  [2]. These higher-mass phthalates—primarily di(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (DEHP), di-n-octyl phthalate (DnOP), di-isononyl phthalate (DiNP), and di-isodecyl phthalate (DiDP)—are extensively employed in polymeric applications such as polyvinyl chloride (PVC), construction materials, flooring, and medical devices, where they supply flexibility, durability, and chemical stability  [1, 8, 9]. As with lower-mass analogs, HMW phthalates are not covalently bonded to polymer matrices but, due to their lower volatility, often exhibit slower but still significant migration into the surrounding environment over time  [4]. This release contributes to human and ecological exposure, particularly through ingestion of contaminated food or water, inhalation of airborne particulate matter, and dermal contact with PVC-containing consumer goods  [2, 6].

Although an overall lower acute toxicity has been frequently attributed to HMW phthalates compared with their short-chain counterparts, specific members of this class—particularly DEHP—retain endocrine-disrupting capacities and have been linked to metabolic disorders, altered blood pressure, and adverse reproductive outcomes, underscoring the importance of meticulous exposure assessment  [2, 9]. Epidemiologic studies have demonstrated the common presence of HMW phthalate metabolites in serum, urine, and even amniotic fluid, highlighting the capacity of these compounds to cross biological barriers, including the placenta  [8, 9]. Consequently, regulatory agencies, particularly in the European Union, impose stringent restrictions on DEHP, DiNP, and other members of this group in toys, food-contact materials, and medical devices, while research pursuits increasingly focus on alternative, less hazardous plasticizers  [4, 8]. Nevertheless, in regions where reforms are limited, HMW phthalates remain extensively used, broadening the scope of worldwide exposure and signaling a continuing need for global standardization in risk assessment and mitigative measures to protect susceptible subpopulations and ecosystems.
### C. Sources of Exposure
Industrial processes, where high-molecular-weight phthalates are essential plasticizers, represent a considerable source of occupational exposure. Workers engaged in polymer manufacturing, product assembly, and related tasks may experience cumulative inhalation of airborne particles and dermal contact with raw materials or dust over prolonged job tenures, thereby increasing their overall phthalate body burden  [10]. Beyond factory settings, extensive research indicates that indoor environments—ranging from residential dwellings to commercial spaces—can harbor substantial phthalate levels in settled dust, generating an ongoing risk, particularly for children, who are prone to frequent hand-to-mouth contact  [5]. In many cases, the slow but consistent release of these compounds from materials like vinyl flooring, carpeting, and other polymeric products contributes to airborne contamination, creating a chronic, low-level inhalation route. Furthermore, consumer product use in everyday life, such as children’s toys, personal care items, and medical devices, adds potential for both dermal and ingestion pathways, illustrating the ubiquitous nature of phthalates in modern indoor environments  [8, 9, 11].

Dietary ingestion further amplifies phthalate exposure risk, since direct contact with food packaging materials and processing equipment can result in chemical migration into meals, beverages, and infant formulas. This is evidenced by analyses of fast-food items that reveal measurable concentrations of phthalates and their replacements, potentially accumulating along the production chain before reaching consumers  [12]. Meanwhile, leaching into soil and water from poorly managed plastic waste or wastewater effluents facilitates bioaccumulation in aquatic systems, agricultural products, and animal tissues, assembling an environmental reservoir capable of sustaining human uptake across diverse settings  [6]. Global reliance on phthalate-containing polymers perpetuates their circulation, as limitations on certain phthalate types—particularly in a few jurisdictions—are often offset by continued use and stocking worldwide, thus heightening the need for vigilant surveillance and responsible disposal methods  [4].
### D. Exposure Pathways
Occupational settings represent a critical source of phthalate exposure due to frequent contact with polymer-based materials, active pharmaceutical ingredients, and process intermediates. In these environments, inhalation of airborne particulates and dermal absorption can occur when tasks involve high-volume mixing or maintenance of production lines. Assessing workplace exposures typically involves establishing occupational exposure limits (OELs), which define thresholds below which most workers will not experience adverse health outcomes. However, there is variability in how different regulatory bodies derive these limits, particularly concerning sensitive endpoints such as respiratory sensitization or developmental toxicity. This heterogeneity highlights the need for accurately characterizing exposure scenarios in each industry, as well as employing suitable biomonitoring and control measures to safeguard worker health and reduce phthalate body burdens. Indeed, meta-analytical insights from various occupational fields emphasize that exposure profiles must be precisely mapped for effectively identifying unacceptable doses. By systematically applying these principles of occupational hygiene risk assessment, employers can adapt their control strategies to eliminate or minimize phthalate releases in the workplace, thereby enhancing workforce protection and long-term well-being  [10, 13, 14].

Beyond industrial contexts, individuals face additional phthalate exposures through everyday activities in enclosed spaces and from consumer products, leading to frequent contact with dust and off-gassed chemicals. Because phthalates are only weakly bound to their substrates, they can readily migrate from items such as flooring materials, furniture finishes, and childcare articles into indoor air. In residential environments, these emissions adhere to dust particles, which are then ingested, inhaled, or transferred across the skin. The potential for elevated intake is especially significant in populations that spend considerable time indoors, including children, who exhibit greater mouthing behaviors, and individuals with underlying health conditions. Likewise, diet continues to be a key contributor when phthalates leach from packaging materials or processing equipment into foods, with fast food and highly processed items often containing some of the highest concentrations of common and replacement plasticizers. Finally, improper disposal of plastic products, coupled with inefficient waste treatment infrastructure, allows phthalates to accumulate in environmental compartments—such as water, soil, or agricultural produce—further broadening the scope of potential human and ecological exposures. This pervasive distribution underscores the necessity of ongoing surveillance, product reformulation, and stringent regulatory guidelines to mitigate widespread phthalate contamination  [5, 6, 9, 12].
## Toxicokinetics (ADME)
Phthalates can be absorbed through ingestion, inhalation, dermal contact, and intravenous routes, with children and pregnant individuals being particularly susceptible through dietary transfer and transplacental exposure  [1, 9]. In general, ingestion serves as a prominent route of exposure for higher molecular weight phthalates, while lower molecular weight congeners exhibit a greater capacity for dermal penetration owing to their smaller molecular radii and relatively higher diffusivity  [1]. Once taken up, phthalates can efficiently distribute via the circulatory system, facilitated by their lipophilicity and partitioning into various tissue compartments.

Following absorption, the initial step in phthalate metabolism is hydrolysis of the diester bond by lipase- and esterase-like enzymes, yielding monoester derivatives that are more biologically active than the parent compounds  [1]. For instance, di(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (DEHP) is converted to mono(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (MEHP), which can undergo subsequent oxidative transformations to form additional metabolites such as mono(2-ethyl-5-oxohexyl) phthalate (MEOHP)  [8, 15]. These secondary products often undergo phase II biotransformations—including conjugation with glucuronic acid—before excretion, with half-lives in the range of several hours to about one day, depending on molecular structure  [1]. Ultimately, the principal pathway of elimination for these polar metabolites is urinary excretion, making urine-based biomonitoring the principal method for quantifying individual body burdens  [11, 13]. Observed restrictions on DEHP in certain jurisdictions highlight the regulatory focus on phthalates as a class; however, the ubiquity of these chemicals, as well as the discovery of novel substitutes, reinforces the necessity of ongoing toxicokinetic investigations  [7].
### A. Absorption Across Exposure Routes
Phthalates can be absorbed through several major routes, each mediated by distinct physicochemical properties and exposure scenarios. Ingestion represents the dominant route of exposure for higher molecular weight phthalates, as these compounds can effectively migrate into food items from packaging or processing materials, then accumulate along the food chain before entering the gastrointestinal tract. In line with this observation, multiple surveys have identified diet as the principal source of human phthalate intake, particularly for compounds such as di(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (DEHP) in meat-based food products. In contrast, lower molecular weight phthalates tend to favor dermal absorption due to their smaller molecular size and relatively enhanced diffusivity through skin layers. Thus, products such as personal care items or cosmetics enriched with low molecular weight phthalates can facilitate significant dermal uptake. Inhalation of airborne phthalates can also be a non-negligible route of exposure in indoor environments, where dust-bound phthalates or volatile emissions from plastics may deposit on mucosal surfaces in the respiratory tract. Although occupational settings may intensify this pathway, household exposure through phthalate-laden dust also remains a source of concern for the general public. Finally, intravenous exposure can arise from medical devices composed of phthalate-containing plastics, a route underscoring the vulnerability of hospital patients undergoing repeated treatment processes. Pregnant women and children are particularly susceptible to these multiple pathways, as dietary transfer, and in some cases, transplacental passage of phthalates, can expose sensitive developing tissues to potential toxic effects. The overarching propensity for phthalates to mobilize from diverse plastic matrices and disperse via ingestion, skin contact, breathing in, or intravenous infusion highlights the need for ongoing efforts to reduce contact with phthalate-rich products while clarifying population-level exposure burdens through biomonitoring.
### B. Metabolic Pathways and Active Metabolites
Phthalate metabolism proceeds via an initial hydrolysis phase, in which hydrolases such as lipase and carboxylesterases cleave diester bonds, yielding monoester phthalates that often exhibit greater bioactivity than the parent diesters  [1, 16]. In the case of di(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (DEHP), hydrolysis generates mono-2-ethylhexyl phthalate (MEHP), which has been shown to bind and modulate peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs), thereby enhancing endocrine-disrupting effects  [15]. Subsequent oxidative modifications can create additional secondary metabolites, further influencing toxicity  [6]. These transformations highlight how chemical structure underlies phthalates’ variable toxicities, as different side-chain lengths and functional groups affect the efficiency of enzyme-mediated hydrolysis and receptor affinity  [2].

In the second stage of metabolism, the monoester and oxidative metabolites formed during hydrolysis typically undergo conjugation by UDP-glucuronosyltransferases, becoming more water-soluble and more readily excreted in urine or bile  [16]. Overall, the production of toxicologically active monoester metabolites, including MEHP, underscores the significance of these pathways in mediating phthalates’ endocrine-disrupting properties. Continuous exposure to phthalate-containing materials thus creates ongoing opportunities for bioactivation of these compounds, inviting broader concern about their health implications across sensitive populations, such as pregnant women and children, who may be more susceptible to the downstream effects of these reactive metabolites.
### C. Excretion and Biomonitoring Indicators
Phthalate excretion follows the second-phase conjugation reaction, whereby the monoester and oxidative metabolites of diester compounds are converted into more hydrophilic derivatives via UDP-glucuronosyltransferases, allowing efficient clearance primarily through the kidneys. However, certain high-molecular-weight phthalates also undergo biliary elimination, underscoring the multifaceted nature of excretion pathways for these endocrine-disrupting chemicals  [16]. In human biomonitoring, the measurement of phthalate metabolites in urine has emerged as a key exposure indicator, given the high detection frequency and sensitivity of analytical methods such as solid-phase extraction coupled with high-performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry  [11, 15]. Concentrations of total DEHP metabolites—often calculated by summing mono-2-ethylhexyl phthalate (MEHP) and other hydroxyl or carboxylated derivatives—are used to index internal DEHP burden in epidemiological research. These biomonitoring approaches account for sample-specific factors such as urinary creatinine or specific gravity to adjust for variable dilution, thereby facilitating robust comparisons across individuals. Additionally, researchers frequently adopt isotope-dilution quantification and batch-specific procedural blanks to ensure accurate results, with sub–parts-per-billion detection limits enabling comprehensive assessment of phthalate exposure in both general and vulnerable populations  [2].
## Molecular Mechanisms of Action
Phthalates are increasingly recognized as pervasive endocrine disruptors that can affect critical hormonal pathways by mimicking, antagonizing, or otherwise modulating endogenous hormone receptors, such as the androgen receptor (AR) or nuclear receptors including peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-α (PPARα) and γ (PPARγ)  [2, 9, 17]. Comprehensive mechanistic studies highlight phthalates’ potential to reduce androgenic signaling through AR antagonism, disrupt testicular steroidogenesis, and impair male reproductive development, in part explaining the constellation of effects termed the “phthalate syndrome,” which includes cryptorchidism, hypospadias, and lower anogenital distance  [6, 18]. Certain phthalates appear less potent than established AR antagonists in eliciting these reproductive effects, yet collectively pose concern when exposure occurs simultaneously with other endocrine-active substances. In addition to their anti-androgenic modes of action, some phthalates are thought to influence the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), although the extent of this cross-talk requires further exploration.

Another significant aspect of phthalate toxicity is the interplay with oxidative stress, mitochondrial function, and epigenetics. Experimental evidence suggests that phthalates can elicit reactive oxygen species generation, compromise mitochondrial enzymes, and alter hepatic or testicular energy metabolism  [19]. Such oxidative disturbances, in tandem with epigenetic modifications (e.g., disrupted DNA methylation or histone modification patterns), raise concerns over transgenerational impacts, wherein phthalate-induced epigenetic signatures may persist into subsequent generations even in the absence of direct exposure  [20]. While the precise molecular cascades remain incompletely understood, it is evident that these compounds exert multifactorial toxicity involving receptor-mediated interference, metabolic dysregulation, and possible heritable epigenetic imprints, underscoring the need for ongoing mechanistic research to inform regulatory policies and mitigate long-term risks.
### A. Endocrine Disruption and Anti-androgenic Effects
Phthalates exert their anti-androgenic effects through multiple pathways, with direct antagonism at the androgen receptor acting as a key mechanism. These compounds appear to compete with endogenous androgens such as dihydrotestosterone for binding to the receptor’s ligand-binding domain, ultimately diminishing downstream androgenic signaling and compromising the normal development and function of androgen-dependent tissues  [21]. Although some phthalates are considered weaker AR antagonists individually, concurrent exposure to other endocrine-active chemicals could potentiate these effects, leading to enhanced disruption of male reproductive development. Empirical data also indicate that phthalates modulate testicular steroidogenesis by interfering with crucial enzymes and regulatory proteins, including steroidogenic acute regulatory protein (StAR), cytochrome P450 family members, and 17β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase, thereby reducing testosterone biosynthesis  [16]. This deficit in hormone production is particularly consequential during the fetal masculinization programming window, when androgen signaling is indispensable for normal testicular differentiation.

The net outcome of these AR-mediated and steroidogenic disturbances includes abnormal testicular development, decreased anogenital distance, and malformations such as hypospadias and cryptorchidism, culminating in the so-called “phthalate syndrome.” Emerging research further suggests potential additive or synergistic interactions with co-exposures to other endocrine-disrupting compounds, a finding that underscores the complexity of assessing phthalate toxicities in real-world contexts  [22]. Through these overlapping and interrelated pathways, phthalates compromise normal hormonal homeostasis, raising persistent questions about the implications for human health and development. Additional mechanistic studies are warranted to elucidate how these agents collectively alter androgen receptor function, steroid hormone biosynthesis, and tissue-specific programming, contributing to multifaceted pathophysiological outcomes over the life course.
### B. Nuclear Receptor Modulation (PPARs, AhR)
Beyond their antagonistic actions on the androgen receptor, phthalates also modulate other nuclear receptor frameworks that govern essential physiological processes. One particularly important class of targets includes the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs), which function as lipid sensors and transcriptional regulators of metabolic genes. Phthalate metabolites such as monobutyl phthalate and mono(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate can bind and activate PPAR isoforms, triggering downstream effects on fatty acid metabolism and potentially disturbing normal lipid homeostasis. These alterations may be especially consequential in highly metabolically active tissues such as the liver and adipose depots, where PPAR-α and PPAR-γ signaling pathways are critical for sustaining energy balance. Experimental data indicate that the ligand-dependent binding of phthalates to PPAR complexes shifts the transcriptional activity of numerous target genes, thereby influencing lipid transport, β-oxidation, and storage patterns in ways that could exacerbate endocrine disruption. Although distinct PPAR subtypes exhibit tissue-specific functions, their shared dependence on heterodimerization with the retinoid X receptor and recruitment of various coactivators and corepressors highlights the complexity of phthalate-induced transcriptional changes. Phthalates’ interference with these delicate nuclear receptor networks may thus complement or reinforce anti-androgenic signaling, amplifying the overall endocrine-disrupting outcomes  [2, 15, 23, 24].

In parallel with PPAR dysregulation, the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR)—another ligand-activated transcription factor—has also been implicated in cross talk with multiple endocrine receptors. Although the precise interactions between phthalates and AhR remain to be fully elucidated, emerging evidence highlights that exposure to diverse endocrine-disrupting chemicals can perturb AhR-dependent transcriptional programs. Studies suggest that AhR activation might modulate expression of steroidogenic enzymes and other nuclear receptor pathways, thereby amplifying the hormonal impacts initiated by PPAR- and AR-focused interference. The combined or sequential engagement of these receptor systems potentially contributes to epigenetic modifications in target cells, increasing the likelihood of persistent alterations that span developmental windows. Consequently, phthalate-driven PPAR and AhR perturbations constitute a significant dimension of systemic hormonal disruption, underscoring the importance of integrative research that dissects mechanistic convergence across nuclear receptor signaling networks  [22].
### C. Oxidative Stress and Mitochondrial Dysfunction
Phthalate exposure has been repeatedly linked to excess reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, particularly through mitochondrial dysfunction in tissues such as the liver and testes. These chemicals can impair the electron transport chain, causing partial reduction of oxygen and the consequent generation of superoxide anions, hydrogen peroxide, or hydroxyl radicals, substances widely recognized as pathological triggers in oxidative stress. While low levels of ROS serve essential cellular signaling roles, heightened intracellular concentrations provoke oxidative damage to lipids, proteins, and DNA, collectively exacerbating inflammatory responses and compromising normal metabolic processes. This imbalance between the production and detoxification of ROS upsets redox homeostasis, thereby signaling inflammatory cascades, enhancing peroxide-driven chain reactions, and ultimately promoting cellular injury. Mitochondria are central to these processes because oxidative phosphorylation is a major site of ROS generation, and chemical insult by phthalates can augment ROS output by hampering efficient electron flow and deregulating redox cycling in these organelles. Such disruption may also feed into apoptotic pathways, where mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization and subsequent caspase activation culminate in programmed cell death. Phthalates, therefore, can foster a pro-oxidant intracellular environment, creating a cycle of continued oxidative stress and mitochondrial compromise, which in turn contributes to morbidity in target tissues and may amplify their endocrine-disrupting properties through altered nuclear receptor function and epigenetic modifications, as discussed in preceding sections. Consequently, these mechanistic insights clarify how phthalates’ oxidative stress-inducing tendencies converge at the nexus of receptor dysregulation and inflammatory signaling, potentially underscoring long-term detrimental health outcomes for exposed individuals  [1, 25, 26].
## Organ-Specific Toxicity (Evidence Review)
Phthalate exposure exerts substantial reproductive toxicity, as evidenced by in vivo studies demonstrating deleterious effects on testicular function and fertility parameters including reduced sperm quality and disrupted hormonal profiles. These outcomes are attributed to both direct interference with gonadal steroidogenesis and mitochondrial oxidative stress, which can impair spermatogenesis and activate apoptotic pathways. Such mechanisms are underscored by the capacity of phthalates to perturb metabolic and epigenetic processes in the reproductive tract, as reflected in systematic reviews that link phthalate-induced mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and altered nuclear receptor signaling to adverse fertility outcomes in animal models and potentially in humans  [16, 27]. Furthermore, paternal exposure to endocrine-disrupting compounds has been shown to induce heritable epigenetic modifications, suggesting that male germ-line alterations may contribute to transgenerational risks, even when overt toxicity is not immediately apparent  [19].

Developmental processes may also be susceptible to phthalate-induced toxicity. While substantial empirical data focus on reproductive endpoints, there is increasing concern that phthalates, like other endocrine disruptors, could cross the placental barrier and interfere with embryonic development. Although fewer rigorous studies have examined phthalate-related neurodevelopmental outcomes in mammals, existing lines of evidence on endocrine disruptors support a plausible risk of dysregulated neuronal maturation and synaptic function, requiring further longitudinal research to clarify potential effects on behavior and cognition. In the liver, phthalate exposure can intensify metabolic stress and exacerbate oxidative injury by impairing mitochondrial electron transport, amplifying reactive oxygen species generation, and disrupting normal lipid and carbohydrate processing. These disturbances are often partnered with diminished antioxidant defenses and inflammatory signaling cascades, which compound cellular damage and may promote fibrosis or steatosis if exposures persist  [25]. Altogether, multiple reports confirm that acute and chronic phthalate exposures pose significant risks to reproductive, developmental, metabolic, and hepatic health, aligning with broader concerns that endocrine-disrupting chemicals exert multifactorial toxicity in target organs  [17].
### A. Reproductive and Developmental Outcomes
Phthalates are recognized endocrine disruptors that can compromise normal hormone regulation across multiple reproductive processes  [17, 22]. Evidence from in vitro and in vivo studies indicates that they hamper steroidogenesis by interfering with critical pathways, such as cAMP–protein kinase A signaling, leading to diminished testosterone production and perturbations in other sex hormones  [27]. Moreover, phthalates adversely affect sperm quality, decreasing motility, altering morphological integrity, and instigating DNA damage in spermatozoa  [1]. Underlying mechanisms include heightened mitochondrial oxidative stress, which emboldens apoptotic pathways and undermines the supportive function of Sertoli cells, ultimately impairing spermatogenesis  [6]. In parallel, such disruptions in testicular function can lead to broader perturbations in gonadal homeostasis, further contributing to subfertility or infertility.

Beyond these disruptions in male reproductive physiology, phthalates can also cross the placenta, exposing the developing fetus to potential harm  [9, 15]. Rodent and other mammalian models reveal that early-life phthalate exposures compromise embryonic development, reducing blastocyst formation rates and interfering with normal cellular fate decisions  [28]. Extensive research further suggests that phthalates induce heritable epigenetic modifications, including changes in DNA methylation and chromatin remodeling, with transgenerational effects that manifest in offspring long after the initial exposure has ceased  [20, 29].
### B. Neurodevelopmental Associations
Experimental and epidemiological evidence increasingly points to phthalates as potent disruptors of neurodevelopmental integrity, with prenatal and early-life exposures influencing the vulnerability of the developing brain. Studies have shown that certain phthalate esters such as dibutyl phthalate (DBP) can traverse both the placental and blood–brain barriers, facilitating direct impacts on neural cells. In vitro work in murine cortical neurons has demonstrated that DBP exposure triggers robust apoptosis through the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) pathway, culminating in increased oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction. By contrast, this apoptotic mechanism is not mediated by estrogen receptors or peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma. Such findings underscore the complexity of phthalate-induced neuronal injury, which often manifests during critical windows of embryonic or postnatal brain development, leaving profound neurotoxic and neurobehavioral sequelae that extend into later life  [30].

Observational data in humans support these laboratory insights, revealing adverse cognitive and behavioral outcomes linked to prenatal phthalate exposures. Specifically, decreased mental and motor development scores, greater prevalence of internalizing behaviors, and social functioning deficits in children have all been associated with higher maternal phthalate burdens during gestation. These clinical correlations suggest that phthalates, by interfering with neurotrophic factors and steroidogenic pathways, may program long-lasting changes in the fetal brain’s developmental trajectory. Epigenetic modifications, including altered DNA methylation and histone remodeling in genes essential for neurodevelopment, are increasingly implicated in this process, presenting a plausible biological mechanism for how transgenerational effects can persist beyond the initial exposure event. Moveover, recent investigations highlight that phthalates may awaken or reinforce these epigenetic patterns in both maternal and paternal germline cells, perpetuating potential neurodevelopmental risk across multiple generations  [15, 19, 20, 31].
### C. Metabolic and Hepatic Effects
Phthalates are highly lipophilic chemicals that can accumulate in adipose tissue after environmental exposure, contributing to significant metabolic and hepatic disturbances over time  [32]. A growing body of evidence suggests that phthalates elicit hepatic inflammation through multiple pathways, including the induction of oxidative stress and disruption of normal immune function via proinflammatory cytokine activation. In particular, downstream metabolites such as mono(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate can bind and activate peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors in hepatic and adipocyte lineages, thereby altering lipid metabolism and perpetuating a state of dysregulated energy homeostasis  [15]. Parallel findings link di(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate to insulin resistance, obesity, and related cardiometabolic disorders, underscoring the broad-reaching consequences of chronic phthalate exposure on metabolic health  [2].

Experimental investigations also reveal that chronic exposure to certain phthalates exacerbates fatty liver conditions by intensifying inflammatory signaling cascades and mitochondrial dysfunction, making steatotic livers more susceptible to ischemia–reperfusion injury and necrosis  [33]. These pathophysiological changes frequently involve endoplasmic reticulum stress and consequent activation of the ROS–NLRP3inflammasome axis, culminating in robust proinflammatory recruitment that damages hepatic parenchyma. Moreover, phthalates’ capacity to disrupt normal endocrine pathways extends to key hormonal regulators of metabolism, thereby amplifying hepatic lipid accumulation and potentiating the risk of nonalcoholic fatty liver disease. Taken together, these mechanistic insights highlight the critical need to characterize phthalate-induced hepatic injury in diverse populations and to consider regulatory measures that minimize exposures with the potential to accelerate the progression of metabolic syndromes.
## Vulnerable Populations
During the first 1000 days of life, encompassing both prenatal and early postnatal development, the fetus and neonate are particularly vulnerable to the adverse effects of phthalates. These chemicals, including higher-molecular-weight variants, can readily cross the placental barrier, subjecting the developing fetus to direct exposure as they accumulate in circulating blood and potentially in amniotic fluid. Studies have shown that measurable levels of phthalate metabolites in maternal biospecimens correlate with adverse birth outcomes, such as preterm birth and reduced fetal growth parameters, underscoring the importance of mitigating prenatal exposure. Moreover, molecular investigations reveal that prenatal phthalate exposure can alter the placental transcriptome and immune milieu, potentially contributing to persistent health consequences later in childhood. The capacity of phthalates to perturb the maternal–fetal interface raises significant concerns about their implications for liver development and metabolism in utero, particularly given their observed capacity to elicit inflammatory and oxidative stress pathways during critical windows of organogenesis  [1, 9, 15, 34].

In addition to the placental route, lactation provides a further avenue through which newborns may be exposed to phthalates. Postnatal transmission via breast milk can be appreciable, especially when maternal exposures are high or chronic, given the lipophilic nature of many phthalate derivatives. Although breastfeeding is broadly encouraged for its myriad immunologic and nutritional benefits, these findings highlight the need for continuous monitoring of environmental contaminants in lactating mothers. In populations with heightened cumulative exposure, phthalate levels found in breast milk may contribute to the early-life onset of metabolic and hepatic dysregulation. These insights emphasize the necessity of reducing maternal exposures in order to safeguard neonatal health and underscore the broader imperative of instituting targeted policy interventions that account for the susceptibility of infants to environmental toxicants during this formative period  [6, 9, 32].
### A. Early Life Exposure Windows
During early life exposure windows, the susceptibility of the developing fetus and neonate is amplified by the dynamic nature of placental transfer mechanisms and the overall immaturity of metabolic pathways. Phthalates can cross the placental barrier via transcellular processes such as passive diffusion, active transport, and pinocytosis, thereby facilitating direct fetal exposure even in the first trimester when placental development is not yet fully complete  [35]. This physiologic interface is underpinned by transporter proteins and the specialized structure of syncytiotrophoblast cells, which can both facilitate and mitigate phthalate movement toward fetal circulation. However, incomplete placental architecture in early gestation may offer only partial protection, underscoring the heightened vulnerability of embryonic and fetal tissues to potentially toxic perturbations in these initial stages.

Empirical research has also revealed that maternal body burden of certain phthalates can exceed thresholds of concern, with some studies identifying high-dose maternal exposures that adversely affect fetal outcomes. For instance, di-n-butyl phthalate (DBP) levels above 100 mg/kg body weight/day in rodent models have been shown to impair fetal development, prompting questions about translational implications for human pregnancies  [32]. Although human exposure typically remains below the established no-observed-adverse-effect levels and tolerable daily intakes, repeated or cumulative exposures during sensitive windows—such as the early months of pregnancy—raise legitimate concerns about subtle developmental effects on metabolic, immune, and neural systems. As maternal and neonatal susceptibility converge in this period, understanding exposure thresholds and implementing strategies to reduce or prevent elevated maternal phthalate loads is imperative for safeguarding offspring health.
### B. Maternal–Fetal and Lactational Transfer
Maternal–fetal transfer of phthalates involves intricate biological processes that operate within a uniquely dynamic interface. While the placenta provides a partial safety net against xenobiotics by means of export pumps, cytochrome P450 enzymes, and a carefully structured syncytiotrophoblast layer, these protective mechanisms are neither absolute nor uniformly effective during all stages of gestation  [36]. Early in pregnancy, limited placental development and incomplete villous architecture permit more unfettered transport of exogenous agents, including phthalates, from maternal circulation to the fetus, thus heightening embryonic vulnerability  [35]. Moreover, variations in phthalate chemical properties, such as lipophilicity and molecular size, enable passive diffusion or active cargo-mediated translocation across the syncytiotrophoblast barrier, facilitating direct fetal exposure during critical developmental windows when metabolic detoxification systems in the fetus are still maturing  [35].

Simultaneously, maternal phthalate burden can escalate—from continual low-dose exposures in everyday consumer products to more substantial concentrations in certain environments—raising the possibility that these compounds can traverse the placental interface in quantities sufficient to alter fetal development  [15, 34]. The immature fetal liver and other enzymatic pathways may accordingly be less able to process and excrete these toxicants, which can produce cumulative and potentially synergistic disruptions of endocrine, neural, and immunological functions in the developing fetus. In addition, lactational transfer further extends the exposure period beyond birth, especially considering that lipophilic chemicals, including specific phthalate congeners, can partition into breast milk and potentially exert biological effects on the neonate  [9]. While ingestion generally remains the primary route of phthalate uptake, lactational exposure underscores the need to consider maternal body burden not only as a prenatal hazard but also as a continued postnatal risk factor for offspring  [32]. These intertwined maternal–fetal and lactational pathways reiterate the importance of systematic exposure assessments and targeted mitigation strategies tailored to these most vulnerable life stages.
## Regulatory Landscape & Tolerable Limits
Regulatory agencies worldwide have developed reference values to limit phthalate exposures with the overarching goal of minimizing adverse health outcomes over a lifetime. In the United States, the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) commonly employs the Reference Dose (RfD) framework, which is derived from a point of departure—often a lowest-observed-adverse-effect level—using conservative uncertainty factors to address interspecies extrapolation, human variability, and other data gaps  [37, 38]. By contrast, the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) and the European Chemicals Agency (ECHA) often rely on the Tolerable Daily Intake (TDI), which similarly incorporates safety margins but is typically applied to broader categories of chemical agents that may accumulate in the body over time  [38]. Although both RfD and TDI methodologies weigh toxicodynamic and toxicokinetic uncertainties, some differences arise in the underlying hazard identification processes and in the magnitudes of safety factors assigned. For phthalates, the TDI may also reflect considerations of cumulative body burden from multiple exposure pathways, particularly in critical reproductive or developmental windows  [6].

Because phthalates frequently co-occur in consumer products, food, and the environment, regulatory approaches are increasingly shifting toward cumulative risk assessment to account for combined or mixture toxicity. Traditional single-chemical risk assessments may underestimate the potential harm arising from simultaneous exposures to multiple phthalate congeners. To address this complexity, mixture toxicity evaluations can incorporate dose-addition models, wherein the potencies of individual phthalates are mathematically scaled and summed to provide an overall risk index for a mixture  [27, 38]. These models account for the possibility that each agent exerts its effects through a convergent mechanism, such as interference with endocrine signaling. Monte Carlo or other probabilistic methods are also employed to characterize uncertainty in complex exposure scenarios, enhancing the robustness of risk characterization when applied at the population level  [39]. Together, these approaches underscore the need to refine regulatory limits and risk evaluations by incorporating mixture-based paradigms—thereby offering more protective guidelines for vulnerable groups, including pregnant women and young infants.
### A. Reference Doses and Tolerable Daily Intakes
Globally, regulators utilize quantitative risk assessment frameworks to derive reference doses (RfDs) and Tolerable Daily Intakes (TDIs) for phthalates, grounding decisions in a systematic progression of hazard identification, hazard characterization, exposure assessment, and risk characterization  [13, 38]. Hazard identification typically establishes whether the chemical poses a potential for adverse effects, after which dose–response data are used to identify a point of departure (such as a NOAEL or LOAEL). To account for extrapolation across species and inter-individual differences in susceptibility, regulators apply uncertainty factors, sometimes subdivided into toxicokinetic and toxicodynamic domains  [37]. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency’s RfD approach frequently employs additional layers of conservative assumptions, whereas the European TDI framework often integrates broader protective margins, reflecting distinctions in allometric scaling and safety factor selection  [14].

In the derivation of reference values for phthalates, interspecies extrapolation can be refined through models that compare NOAELs from subacute and subchronic studies in animals with potential human exposures, sometimes relying on metabolic rate adjustments. This calibration helps characterize the remaining variability after allometric adjustments for body weight or respiratory volume, an especially important step for diverse compounds like phthalates that may be metabolized via multiple pathways  [14]. Overall, these strategies reflect a deliberate endeavor to combine robust data, clear definitions of critical effects, and carefully chosen safety margins, thereby ensuring that reference values for phthalates extend sufficient protection across different demographic groups and life stages.
### B. Cumulative Risk Assessment and Mixture Models
Cumulative risk assessment for phthalates acknowledges that human populations are seldom exposed to a single compound in isolation, and instead encounter complex mixtures characterized by overlapping metabolic pathways and possible interactive effects. Traditional risk evaluations predicated on substance-by-substance approaches can underestimate or overestimate potential hazards, particularly in scenarios where synergistic or antagonistic interactions alter the overall biological impact. Methodologically, researchers have advanced beyond conventional linear additive models by employing innovative statistical tools—such as Bayesian kernel machine regression (BKMR) and random forests—to detect and quantify intricate, non-linear dependencies among multiple chemicals. These approaches allow investigators to evaluate the joint risk of exposure to multiple phthalates more accurately while taking into account factors like demographic covariates, non-monotonic dose–response relationships, and unknown confounders, thereby providing a more holistic view of mixture toxicity  [40].

One commonly used approach for the aggregated risk of phthalate mixtures is the Hazard Index (HI) method, in which individual hazard quotients are summed to yield an overall index of potential non-cancer health risks. By comparing the HI to a unitless benchmark of concern, regulators can ascertain whether combined exposures exceed existing margin-of-safety thresholds for the general population. Despite its utility and practicality, this summation approach assumes additivity among components, which may not fully capture interaction patterns in certain scenarios. As such, experts increasingly recommend augmenting the HI with methods that characterize potential nonadditive toxicological relationships and disentangle the confounding influences of coexposures. Guidance from various agencies encourages researchers to integrate uncertainty analysis into mixture models, thereby recognizing the variability in exposures and multiple metabolic pathways of phthalates. Monte Carlo simulations and higher-tier probabilistic frameworks can further refine cumulative risk characterizations by incorporating the full distribution of exposures and individual susceptibilities. These developments reflect ongoing efforts to safeguard diverse populations—including vulnerable groups such as children and pregnant women—through reference values and methodological advances that align with the reality of phthalate coexposure  [2, 13, 38].
## Emerging Science & Alternatives
As concerns about phthalates intensify, researchers and industry stakeholders have begun to explore the potential benefits and drawbacks of alternative plasticizers such as di(2-ethylhexyl) terephthalate (DEHT), commonly referred to as DEHT or DOTP, tris(2-ethylhexyl) trimellitate (TOTM), and diisononyl hexahydrophthalate (DINCH)  [4, 41]. TOTM, for instance, has seen widespread application in medical equipment in Japan, highlighting its perceived suitability in sensitive sectors; similarly, DINCH has been adopted in certain blood collection tubes due to its relatively low acute toxicity profile  [41]. However, preliminary evidence suggests that the long-term health impacts of each of these substitutes require further clarification, particularly regarding possible hepatotoxic or endocrine-disrupting properties at higher exposure levels. Although short-term tests have demonstrated minimal toxicity for DINCH and TOTM, measured increases in DINCH metabolites in human populations over time underscore the possibility of escalating exposures in broader communities  [41]. Moreover, population-based biomonitoring from the United States also points to expanding use of novel plasticizers, whose clinical and epidemiological risk profiles still remain under investigation  [12].

Because extensive toxicological data are not always generated prior to large-scale substitution, experts caution against the possibility of “regrettable substitutions,” wherein new chemicals may exhibit adverse effects comparable to those of the phthalates they replace. In some recent evaluations, TOTM and DINCH exhibited noteworthy similarities to banned phthalates on indices of developmental toxicity, although the magnitude and public health significance of these findings are still debated  [41]. Subtle, non-acute effects—such as hormone modulations—are particularly concerning for children and other vulnerable groups, reinforcing the need for refined screening assays and longitudinal epidemiological studies that can detect subtle or cumulative risks. Consequently, many regulatory bodies advocate that industries adopt a precautionary approach by integrating rigorous in vitro studies, high-throughput screening tools, and post-market surveillance strategies to better detect potential hazards that might not be immediately evident. Through this integrated strategy, it becomes feasible to address human exposure to emerging plasticizers in a transparent manner while balancing the durability and cost requirements that drive industry adoption of novel compounds.
### A. Overview of Alternative Plasticizers
Efforts to replace conventional phthalates with compounds such as di(2-ethylhexyl)terephthalate (DEHT), tris(2-ethylhexyl)trimellitate (TOTM), and diisononyl hexahydrophthalate (DINCH) have been driven by concerns over the established endocrine-disrupting properties and documented reproductive toxicity of legacy phthalates. Although DEHT differs structurally from ortho-phthalates, its widespread adoption has been accompanied by studies showing relatively low acute toxicity in animal models; nonetheless, research on long-term exposure in humans remains limited, highlighting a persistent data gap on chronic health outcomes  [41]. TOTM has become prevalent in Japan for medical applications, favored for its reduced volatility and potentially lower migration rates; however, in rodent models, TOTM exposure prompted pathological changes in the liver, including vascular congestion and lipid accumulation, along with significant alterations in gene expression profiles  [41]. Clinical exposures also appear non-negligible, as various hospital settings incorporate TOTM-based medical devices for pregnant women, underscoring the importance of understanding subtle effects in vulnerable populations  [42].

DINCH, meanwhile, is commonly employed in blood collection and nutrient solution bags due to its perceived lower toxicity in acute and subacute studies. Nonetheless, rising DINCH metabolite levels observed in human biomonitoring programs point to a growing real-world exposure, with detection frequencies increasing markedly over the last two decades  [12, 41]. These trends, coupled with emerging evidence that both TOTM and DINCH may share certain mechanistic or toxicological parallels with banned phthalates, evoke concerns of “regrettable substitution,” in which an ostensibly safer alternative eventually reveals similar risks  [41]. The literature thus underscores a need for more rigorous, long-term evaluative studies and consistent regulatory oversight to ensure that proposed alternatives provide a genuine reduction in risk while maintaining material performance and clinical utility.
### B. Safety and Toxicological Profiles of Substitutes
Alternative plasticizers such as DEHT, TOTM, and DINCH have gained prominence in medical and consumer applications due to their perceived lower toxicity compared to legacy phthalates. DEHT, for instance, is structurally distinct from ortho-phthalates and undergoes rapid hydrolysis in vivo to generate terephthalic acid and 2-ethylhexanol, thereby limiting the formation of more toxic monoesters over time  [12, 43]. While short-term studies on DEHT indicate relatively mild or minimal toxicological outcomes, definitive conclusions about its safety cannot be drawn in the absence of longitudinal data that capture chronic exposure scenarios. Even in vitro investigations hint at latent endocrine-disrupting properties, underscoring the need for ongoing vigilance and rigorous toxicological scrutiny.

TOTM, an ester of trimellitic acid often used in medical devices in Japan, displays clear structural distinctions from phthalate plasticizers. Nonetheless, rodent models have revealed hepatic vascular congestion, lipid globule accumulation, and decreased mitochondrial membrane antigen reactivity in hepatocytes following TOTM exposure, raising concerns about its liver toxicity  [41]. High-dose administration in mice has further shown that TOTM can modulate the expression of hundreds of genes in hepatic tissues, suggesting that it may affect multiple biochemical pathways. Given its utility in clinical settings where patients, including pregnant women, may experience repeated exposures, TOTM’s toxicological profile highlights the importance of strict surveillance and precautionary risk assessments.

DINCH has similarly emerged as a favored substitute in products such as blood collection tubes and nutrient solution bags, with manufacturers promoting it as a substantially less harmful alternative. Acute and subacute testing in rodent models often demonstrate low toxicity, yet biomonitoring studies that measure DINCH metabolites reveal a rising trend in population-level exposure over recent years  [12, 41]. This upward trajectory in measurable DINCH metabolites prompts concern for potential long-term health impacts, especially in vulnerable populations. The current body of evidence for DEHT, TOTM, and DINCH suggests that even structurally dissimilar replacements can present unforeseen biological effects, reinforcing the notion that regulated, comprehensive, and long-term toxicological evaluations are essential for ensuring genuine safety in medical and other high-exposure contexts.
## Conclusion & Future Directions
In conclusion, the breadth of scientific data underscores that phthalate toxicity is multifaceted and persists as a global public health and regulatory concern. Cumulative exposure, particularly to phthalate mixtures, presents an ongoing challenge to traditional single-compound risk assessment frameworks, as evidenced by work linking combined phthalate exposures to adverse birth outcomes and altered reproductive phenotypes  [34]. The emerging literature on transgenerational epigenetic effects further complicates these risk profiles by suggesting that certain environmentally induced molecular signatures can be passed across generations, leading to heritable changes in phenotype without direct exposure  [19, 29]. Although current epidemiological efforts have provided critical insights into associations with metabolic and reproductive dysfunction, important long-term follow-up studies are still needed to establish causal links and to clarify mechanistic pathways in humans  [15, 16, 32]. In parallel, robust clinical biomarkers of early phthalate-related toxicity remain under development, which limits precise estimations of subclinical and cumulative risks  [6].

Moving forward, the shifting landscape of plasticizer usage demands additional research to ensure that alternative products such as DEHT, TOTM, and DINCH do not pose significant hidden hazards. Although these substitutes often bypass some of the recognized toxicological pathways of traditional phthalates, mounting evidence reveals that they may interact with biological systems in unforeseen ways, potentially influencing liver function, reproductive outcomes, or other organ-level processes  [12, 41, 42]. Wide adoption of such replacements in clinical and consumer products highlights the importance of leveraging animal, in vitro, and high-throughput screening models to better anticipate bioactivity and identify hazards early. On top of these investigative needs, global harmonization and regulatory coherence remain paramount. Regulatory agencies currently differ in methodologies and acceptable exposure thresholds, which can hinder effective risk management strategies  [14]. By integrating advanced epidemiological approaches, mechanistic toxicology, and standardized regulatory guidance, the scientific community will be better positioned to safeguard human health in the face of dynamic chemical use patterns and evolving environmental exposures.
### A. Summary of Key Findings
Phthalates, as a class of endocrine-disrupting chemicals, pose significant global public health risks, with growing evidence highlighting both overt and subclinical toxicity across diverse physiological systems. A key insight revolves around complex mixture effects, which challenge traditional single-chemical risk assessments and underscore the real-world scenario in which humans are exposed to multiple phthalates concurrently, often at low doses over extended periods  [2]. Compounding these concerns, studies of epigenetically mediated generational inheritance demonstrate that parental exposure to phthalates and similar toxicants can produce sustained changes in gamete epigenomes, culminating in altered phenotypes in subsequent generations  [20, 29, 44]. Although such findings remain to be fully explored in human populations, they highlight the intricate mechanisms by which chemical exposures can affect reproductive, metabolic, and developmental outcomes beyond the directly exposed individual.

From an epidemiological perspective, phthalate exposure has been linked to adverse reproductive and metabolic endpoints, including disrupted hormonal profiles, obstetric complications, and dysregulated lipid metabolism  [34]. However, establishment of precise causal relationships requires further longitudinal data spanning critical windows of human development, as well as intensive analysis of subclinical risks and cumulative exposures over a lifetime  [27]. Meanwhile, questions persist about the safety of alternative plasticizers, such as di(2-ethylhexyl)terephthalate, tris(2-ethylhexyl)trimellitate, and diisononyl hexahydrophthalate, given the possibility that these may also elicit unforeseen biological interactions  [41]. The use of human-relevant in vitro cell systems and animal models tuned to mechanistic endpoints offers a pragmatic path toward identifying early biomarkers of toxicity and clarifying dose–response thresholds that may be missed by traditional testing frameworks  [45]. In sum, these findings collectively underscore the importance of integrated scientific approaches—combining multifaceted exposure assessments, mechanistic toxicology, and harmonized international guidelines—to better safeguard public health amid expanding global chemical usage.
### B. Research Needs and Policy Implications
Rigorous and integrative research efforts remain critical to fill current knowledge gaps surrounding phthalate toxicity, particularly regarding low-dose, chronic exposures and their potential to exert transgenerational effects. As phthalate mixtures better represent real-world scenarios, applying standardized methodologies in mechanistic toxicity testing will be vital for more reliable dose–response relationships in both in vitro and in vivo models, thereby elucidating potential interactive or cumulative impacts on health  [45-47]. Cross-disciplinary collaboration is likewise needed to leverage expertise from epidemiology, molecular biology, and public health for comprehensive data collection and analysis that capture early indicators of toxicity among diverse populations  [48]. At the same time, guidelines for consistent biomonitoring must be continually adapted to address evolving chemical substitutions and to ensure harmonized assessment protocols that accurately reflect cumulative exposures  [49].

On the policy level, governing bodies will need to update regulatory frameworks to consider mixture effects and to incorporate precautionary approaches that acknowledge social, biological, and developmental vulnerabilities in the population  [4, 37, 38, 39, 41]. Future guidance should also embrace emerging findings on epigenetic mechanisms by integrating evidence of heritable risks into risk assessments, prompting earlier interventions to protect both current and subsequent generations  [19, 31]. A joint commitment—among scientists, regulatory agencies, and industry—toward unified standards, robust longitudinal studies, and inclusion of vulnerable populations will ensure that relevant toxicological information translates effectively into policies aimed at preventing avoidable exposures and safeguarding public health.
