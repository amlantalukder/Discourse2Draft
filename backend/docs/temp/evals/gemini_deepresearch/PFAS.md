# Health Impacts of Per- and Polyfluoroalkyl Substances (PFASs): A Comprehensive Review

**Abstract**

Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFASs) constitute a vast and heterogenous class of anthropogenic fluorinated chemicals that have permeated global ecosystems, industrial supply chains, and human populations since the mid-20th century. Distinguished by the extraordinary strength of the carbon-fluorine bond, these compounds—often termed "forever chemicals"—exhibit unique physicochemical properties including simultaneous hydrophobicity and lipophobicity, high thermal stability, and profound resistance to metabolic and environmental degradation. This comprehensive review synthesizes the current state of toxicological and epidemiological science regarding the health impacts of "legacy" long-chain PFASs, particularly perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA) and perfluorooctane sulfonic acid (PFOS), alongside emerging replacement chemistries such as hexafluoropropylene oxide dimer acid (HFPO-DA, or "GenX") and perfluorobutane sulfonic acid (PFBS).

We critically evaluate the convergence of evidence that has precipitated recent paradigm shifts in regulatory science, most notably the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) 2025 reclassification of PFOA as a Group 1 human carcinogen and the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency’s (EPA) establishment of near-zero Maximum Contaminant Level Goals (MCLGs). The review provides an exhaustive analysis of system-specific health endpoints, identifying immunotoxicity—specifically the suppression of vaccine response—dyslipidemia, developmental deficits, and renal and testicular malignancies as critical hazards. Furthermore, we explore the profound toxicokinetic disparities between human and animal models, particularly regarding renal resorption mechanisms and serum half-lives, which complicate interspecies extrapolation and risk assessment. The analysis extends to the "regrettable substitution" phenomenon observed with short-chain and ether-based alternatives, concluding that structural modification has largely failed to mitigate toxicological risk, necessitating a class-based approach to management and remediation.

## 1. Introduction

The global proliferation of per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFASs) represents one of the most complex and persistent environmental health challenges of the modern era. Since their initial synthesis and commercialization in the 1940s, these chemicals have become ubiquitous in modern society, utilized in applications ranging from aqueous film-forming foams (AFFF) used in firefighting to stain-resistant textiles, non-stick cookware, semiconductor manufacturing, and medical devices. However, the same physicochemical properties that make PFASs industrially indispensable—their thermal stability and chemical inertness—also render them exceptionally persistent in the environment and biological systems.<sup>1</sup>

The fundamental chemical moiety defining this class is the perfluoroalkyl chain, where hydrogen atoms on the carbon backbone are replaced by fluorine. The carbon-fluorine (C-F) bond is the strongest single bond in organic chemistry (approximate bond energy of 485 kJ/mol), imparting extreme resistance to hydrolysis, photolysis, and biodegradation.<sup>3</sup> Consequently, PFASs do not break down under typical environmental conditions, leading to their accumulation in water, soil, and living organisms, earning them the moniker "forever chemicals".<sup>4</sup>

Historically, scientific and regulatory scrutiny was largely confined to a narrow subset of "legacy" long-chain compounds, primarily perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA) and perfluorooctane sulfonic acid (PFOS). These eight-carbon (C8) chain molecules were voluntarily phased out by major manufacturers in the United States and Europe in the early 2000s following the revelation of their widespread global distribution and bioaccumulation potential.<sup>2</sup> However, the industrial response to this phase-out involved a transition to structural analogs, including short-chain PFASs (e.g., PFBS) and perfluoroalkyl ether acids (e.g., GenX), often marketed as safer alternatives due to their comparatively shorter serum half-lives in animal models. Emerging toxicological data now suggests that these replacements may exert similar adverse health effects while possessing higher environmental mobility, challenging the assumption that shorter carbon chains equate to reduced hazard.<sup>6</sup>

The scientific understanding of PFAS toxicity has evolved substantially over the past two decades. Early research focused on gross organ effects, such as hepatomegaly (liver enlargement) and peroxisome proliferation in rodents. Contemporary science has revealed subtle, low-dose perturbations in human systems, including endocrine disruption, immunotoxicity, and epigenetic modifications.<sup>8</sup> This evolution in understanding is reflected in the 2024 U.S. EPA National Primary Drinking Water Regulation, which set enforceable Maximum Contaminant Levels (MCLs) for PFOA and PFOS at 4.0 parts per trillion (ppt)—levels that were technologically undetectable just a few decades ago.<sup>10</sup> Simultaneously, the IARC Monograph 135, finalized in 2023 and published in 2025, elevated PFOA to the highest carcinogenic classification, Group 1 (_carcinogenic to humans_), fundamentally altering the legal and public health landscape surrounding these compounds.<sup>1</sup>

This review aims to provide a comprehensive, peer-review-quality synthesis of the human health risks associated with PFAS exposure. It integrates findings from high-quality epidemiological cohorts—such as the C8 Health Project and the GenX Exposure Study—with mechanistic insights from animal toxicology to clarify causal relationships. Particular attention is paid to the discordance between animal and human toxicokinetics, the mechanisms of carcinogenicity and immunotoxicity, and the toxicological implications of the industrial shift toward novel fluorinated ethers.

## 2. Exposure Pathways and Toxicokinetics

A rigorous assessment of PFAS health risks requires a detailed understanding of their fate within the human body. The toxicokinetic profile of perfluoroalkyl acids—specifically how they are absorbed, distributed, bound to proteins, and excreted—differs profoundly between humans and the standard laboratory animals (rats, mice, monkeys) used to assess their safety. This interspecies discordance is a primary source of uncertainty in risk assessment and necessitates careful adjustment when extrapolating animal toxicity data to human health standards.

### 2.1. Routes of Exposure

Human exposure to PFAS is multimodal and ubiquitous. For the general population living outside of specific contamination zones, the primary route of exposure is dietary ingestion. This includes the consumption of fish and seafood that have bioaccumulated PFASs from aquatic environments, as well as crops grown in contaminated soil or irrigated with contaminated water.<sup>2</sup> Food packaging materials, particularly grease-resistant paper and cardboard treated with fluorotelomers, also contribute to dietary intake as precursor compounds can migrate into food and biotransform into stable perfluoroalkyl acids (PFAAs).<sup>11</sup>

However, in communities located near fluorochemical manufacturing facilities (e.g., the mid-Ohio Valley near the Parkersburg works; the Cape Fear River basin near the Fayetteville works) or military installations where AFFF was heavily used, drinking water becomes the dominant source of exposure.<sup>12</sup> In these populations, serum PFAS concentrations can be orders of magnitude higher than the national averages reported in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES).<sup>14</sup> Other routes include the inhalation of indoor dust, which adsorbs PFASs shed from treated carpets and upholstery, and dermal absorption from personal care products, although the latter is generally considered a minor pathway for ionic PFASs due to their repellency properties.<sup>11</sup>

### 2.2. Absorption and Distribution

Following oral ingestion, PFASs are readily absorbed in the gastrointestinal tract. Studies indicate absorption fractions greater than 95% for long-chain compounds like PFOA, PFOS, and PFNA in mammals, suggesting high bioavailability.<sup>16</sup> Unlike traditional persistent organic pollutants (POPs) such as polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) or dioxins, which are lipophilic and partition into adipose tissue, PFASs are proteinophilic.

Once absorbed, PFASs bind non-covalently to plasma proteins, primarily serum albumin.<sup>17</sup> This high affinity for albumin facilitates their transport throughout the body and limits their availability for glomerular filtration. From the blood, PFASs distribute primarily to highly perfused, protein-rich tissues, predominantly the liver, kidneys, and lungs.<sup>18</sup> The volume of distribution ($V_d$) in humans is relatively low (approx. 200 mL/kg), consistent with distribution primarily in the extracellular space and plasma.<sup>19</sup>

Crucially, PFASs are capable of crossing significant biological barriers. They cross the placental barrier, resulting in fetal exposure throughout gestation. Cord blood concentrations of PFOA and PFOS are typically lower than maternal serum levels but are highly correlated, indicating efficient maternal-fetal transfer.<sup>20</sup> Furthermore, these compounds are excreted in breast milk. While breastfeeding serves as a significant route of depuration for the mother—often resulting in lower serum levels in multiparous women—it represents a primary and concentrated source of exposure for the nursing infant. Consequently, breastfed infants often exhibit serum PFAS concentrations that rapidly exceed those of their mothers during the first months of life.<sup>4</sup>

### 2.3. Metabolism: The Stability Problem

A defining toxicological feature of perfluoroalkyl acids is their absolute resistance to metabolic biotransformation. To date, there is no evidence that PFOA, PFOS, PFBS, or PFHxS undergo metabolism in the human body. The strength of the C-F bond prevents enzymatic attack by cytochrome P450 systems or conjugation pathways.<sup>1</sup> This lack of metabolism means that the body relies entirely on excretion to lower internal burdens. Precursor compounds (e.g., fluorotelomer alcohols or sulfonamides) can be metabolized in the liver, but they transform _into_ the stable terminal PFAAs (e.g., PFOA or PFOS), effectively increasing the internal dose of the persistent parent compounds.<sup>21</sup>

### 2.4. Excretion and Elimination Half-Lives

The kinetics of excretion reveal the most critical divergence between humans and experimental animals. In humans, legacy PFASs exhibit multi-year half-lives due to efficient renal reabsorption. PFOA has an estimated arithmetic mean half-life of approximately 2.3 to 3.8 years, while PFOS is even more persistent, with estimated half-lives ranging from 3.4 to 5.4 years.<sup>22</sup> PFHxS, a six-carbon sulfonate, exhibits the longest half-life among commonly monitored PFASs, estimated at 5.3 to 8.5 years.<sup>23</sup>

In stark contrast, the half-life of PFOA in male rats is measured in days, and in female rats, it is merely hours.<sup>22</sup> This massive interspecies difference is attributed to the differential expression and function of Organic Anion Transporters (OATs) in the kidney.

#### 2.4.1. The Role of Renal Resorption

PFASs are filtered from the blood by the glomerulus. However, in humans, OATs located in the proximal tubule cells (specifically OAT1, OAT3, and OAT4) actively resorb these compounds from the urine filtrate back into the blood.<sup>20</sup> Human transport proteins appear to be highly efficient at recognizing and retrieving long-chain PFASs, effectively recycling them within the body. In rodents, these transporters are regulated by sex hormones; for instance, female rats express OATs that facilitate rapid excretion of PFOA, whereas male rats express isoforms that promote retention, though still far less efficiently than humans.<sup>18</sup>

This mechanism has profound implications for toxicity testing. To achieve serum concentrations in rodents that are thermodynamically equivalent to human occupational or environmental burdens, researchers must administer massive external doses. This "high dose" bolus administration can activate pathways (such as rapid peroxisome proliferation) that may not be relevant to the chronic, low-dose accumulation seen in humans, complicating the interpretation of animal bioassays.<sup>24</sup>

## 3. System-Specific Health Impacts

The epidemiological and toxicological evidence links PFAS exposure to a diverse array of adverse health outcomes. While early assessments focused on the liver and developmental effects observed in animals, modern epidemiology has identified the immune system, lipid metabolism, and specific endocrine functions as highly sensitive targets in humans.

### 3.1. Immunotoxicity

Immunotoxicity has emerged as one of the most sensitive and critical endpoints for PFAS risk assessment. Both the U.S. National Toxicology Program (NTP) and the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) have identified immune suppression as a primary hazard driving regulatory limits.<sup>32</sup>

#### 3.1.1. Suppression of Vaccine Response

The most robust epidemiological finding is the association between elevated serum PFAS levels and reduced antibody response to routine vaccinations in children. The NTP’s systematic review concluded that PFOA and PFOS are "presumed to be an immune hazard to humans" based on a high level of evidence from animal studies and a moderate level of evidence from humans.<sup>33</sup>

Specific studies, such as those conducted by Grandjean et al. in the Faroe Islands birth cohort, demonstrated that a doubling of PFOS exposure measured at age 5 was associated with a statistically significant decline (up to 39%) in antibody concentrations against tetanus and diphtheria toxoids at age 7.<sup>20</sup> In some children, antibody titers dropped below the level considered protective, clinically rendering the vaccination ineffective. These findings have been replicated in other cohorts, leading EFSA to use this specific endpoint—decreased response to vaccination—as the critical effect for establishing its group Tolerable Weekly Intake (TWI) in 2020.<sup>36</sup> The use of human data for this endpoint avoids the uncertainties associated with interspecies extrapolation factors.

#### 3.1.2. Susceptibility to Infectious Disease

Corroborating the vaccine response data, observational studies suggest a broader impairment of adaptive immunity. Higher prenatal or childhood PFAS levels have been linked to an increased incidence of common infectious diseases, including lower respiratory tract infections and gastroenteritis.<sup>9</sup>

More recently, the potential for PFASs to exacerbate viral infections has been investigated in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic. A study in Denmark analyzing plasma samples from patients with COVID-19 found that plasma concentrations of perfluorobutanoic acid (PFBA)—a short-chain PFAS that accumulates in the lungs—were associated with an increased risk of severe disease outcomes. The odds ratio for severe COVID-19 was 2.19 (95% CI: 1.39–3.46) for those with elevated PFBA, suggesting that background exposure to these immunotoxicants could be an aggravating factor in pandemics.<sup>39</sup>

#### 3.1.3. Mechanisms of Immune Disruption

Mechanistically, PFASs appear to suppress adaptive immunity through multiple pathways. Animal studies demonstrate atrophy of lymphoid organs (spleen and thymus) and suppression of T-cell dependent antibody responses (TDAR).<sup>27</sup> In vitro models using human immune cells indicate that PFASs can inhibit the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-$\kappa$B) pathway, which is crucial for cytokine production and immune cell proliferation, and induce oxidative stress in lymphocytes.<sup>32</sup>

### 3.2. Carcinogenicity

The evaluation of PFAS carcinogenicity has undergone a significant evolution, culminating in the IARC's 2025 reclassification, which integrated traditional bioassay data with modern mechanistic evidence.

#### 3.2.1. PFOA: Group 1 (Carcinogenic to Humans)

In Monograph 135, the IARC Working Group classified PFOA as _carcinogenic to humans_ (Group 1).<sup>1</sup> This represents a significant upgrade from its previous 2014 classification as _possibly carcinogenic_ (Group 2B). The classification was driven by the convergence of three lines of evidence:

1.  **Animal Evidence:** There is "sufficient" evidence of cancer in experimental animals. PFOA consistently induces a triad of tumors in rats: Leydig cell (testicular) adenomas, pancreatic acinar cell tumors, and hepatocellular (liver) adenomas/carcinomas.<sup>8</sup> The observation of malignant neoplasms in both sexes of a single species in Good Laboratory Practice (GLP) studies met the criteria for sufficiency.
2.  **Mechanistic Evidence:** There is "strong" evidence that PFOA exhibits multiple Key Characteristics of Carcinogens (KCCs) in exposed humans. Specifically, PFOA induces epigenetic alterations (DNA methylation changes), is immunosuppressive, and induces oxidative stress. It also modulates receptor-mediated effects, particularly via PPAR$\alpha$.<sup>8</sup>
3.  **Human Evidence:** There is "limited" evidence in humans for renal cell carcinoma and testicular cancer. While positive associations have been observed, particularly in the highly exposed mid-Ohio valley population (C8 Health Project), issues of chance, bias, or confounding prevented a classification of "sufficient" human evidence.<sup>8</sup> However, the IARC preamble allows for a Group 1 classification when sufficient animal evidence is combined with strong mechanistic evidence in humans, bridging the gap left by epidemiological limitations.

#### 3.2.2. PFOS: Group 2B (Possibly Carcinogenic)

PFOS was classified as _possibly carcinogenic to humans_ (Group 2B).<sup>1</sup>

- **Evidence:** The classification rests primarily on "strong" mechanistic evidence similar to PFOA (epigenetics, immunosuppression, receptor modulation). However, the evidence for cancer in humans was deemed "inadequate," and evidence in experimental animals was "limited," predominantly driven by liver tumors in female rats.<sup>8</sup>

#### 3.2.3. Epidemiology of Kidney and Testicular Cancer

The link to renal cell carcinoma is biologically plausible given that the kidneys are the primary route of elimination and contain transport proteins (OATs) that concentrate PFASs in renal tubular cells, potentially exposing these cells to concentrations far higher than those found in serum.<sup>20</sup> Several large cohorts, including the C8 Health Project (involving ~69,000 residents exposed to PFOA via drinking water), have observed dose-response relationships between cumulative PFOA exposure and kidney cancer incidence.<sup>45</sup> Similarly, testicular cancer associations are supported by both epidemiological data in highly exposed workers and community residents, and the high incidence of Leydig cell tumors in rodent bioassays, although the relevance of rodent Leydig cell tumors to humans is complex due to species-specific endocrine feedback loops.<sup>43</sup>

### 3.3. Developmental and Reproductive Toxicity

PFASs readily traverse the placenta, exposing the developing fetus during critical windows of organogenesis and susceptibility.

#### 3.3.1. Low Birth Weight

One of the most consistent findings in environmental epidemiology is the association between maternal PFAS levels and reduced birth weight. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses indicate that for every 1 ng/mL increase in serum PFOA or PFOS, birth weight decreases by approximately 10 to 20 grams.<sup>46</sup> While a decrement of this magnitude may not be clinically significant for an individual infant, on a population level, it shifts the normal distribution curve of birth weights, leading to a higher prevalence of infants born with low birth weight (<2500g) or small for gestational age (SGA).<sup>48</sup> The exact mechanism is thought to involve interference with placental growth factors and maternal thyroid hormone disruption.

#### 3.3.2. Preeclampsia and Hypertension

Exposure to PFOA and PFOS has been positively associated with pregnancy-induced hypertension and preeclampsia.<sup>48</sup> A study of women in Hangzhou, China, found that each log-unit increase in serum PFOA was associated with an Odds Ratio (OR) of 5.29 (95% CI: 1.05, 26.7) for preeclampsia.<sup>48</sup> The proposed mechanism involves placental dysfunction; PFASs may inhibit trophoblast invasion or alter placental vasculature through oxidative stress and anti-angiogenic pathways.<sup>9</sup>

#### 3.3.3. Fertility and Fecundity

PFAS exposure correlates with reduced fecundity (longer time to pregnancy) and lower sperm quality in males (reduced count and motility).<sup>20</sup> Studies in the Danish National Birth Cohort found that higher PFOS levels were associated with a longer time to pregnancy.<sup>20</sup> However, epidemiological assessments of female fertility are complicated by "reverse causation": women who are nulliparous (have not given birth) have naturally higher PFAS levels because they have not excreted the chemical via childbirth and lactation. This pharmacokinetic confounder makes it difficult to distinguish whether PFASs cause infertility or whether infertility leads to higher PFAS retention.<sup>20</sup>

### 3.4. Metabolic and Endocrine Disruption

#### 3.4.1. Dyslipidemia (The "PFAS Paradox")

A consistent, positive association exists between PFAS exposure and elevated serum cholesterol (total and LDL), even at low exposure levels.<sup>49</sup> This relationship presents a toxicological paradox: in rodents, PFASs act as potent agonists of PPAR$\alpha$, a nuclear receptor whose activation typically lowers cholesterol (the mechanism of action for fibrate drugs). However, in humans, exposure leads to _increases_ in lipids. This discrepancy suggests that the human response may involve different pathways, such as the suppression of hepatocyte nuclear factor 4 alpha (HNF4$\alpha$) or disturbances in bile acid recycling, or that PPAR$\alpha$ activation in humans produces a divergent metabolic phenotype compared to rodents.<sup>50</sup>

#### 3.4.2. Liver Enzyme Alterations

Consistent with their accumulation in the liver, PFOA, PFOS, and PFHxS exposures are associated with elevated liver enzymes, particularly alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), indicative of hepatocellular injury.<sup>4</sup> This mirrors the hepatocellular hypertrophy, necrosis, and steatosis seen in animal models, reinforcing the liver as a primary target organ across species.<sup>29</sup>

#### 3.4.3. Thyroid Function

PFASs act as thyroid disruptors. PFOA and PFOS have been associated with altered thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) and thyroxine (T4) levels.<sup>51</sup> In pregnant women, higher PFAS levels correlate with lower maternal T4, which is critical for fetal neurodevelopment. Mechanisms likely include the displacement of T4 from transport proteins (e.g., transthyretin) or enhanced hepatic clearance of thyroid hormones via the induction of glucuronidation enzymes.<sup>52</sup>

## 4. Mechanisms of Action (MOA)

Elucidating the molecular mechanisms of PFAS toxicity is challenging due to the structural diversity of the class and profound species-specific responses. However, recent research has coalesced around several key pathways that extend beyond simple receptor binding.

### 4.1. Peroxisome Proliferator-Activated Receptor Alpha (PPAR$\alpha$)

The most extensively studied mechanism is the activation of PPAR$\alpha$, a nuclear receptor regulating lipid metabolism and inflammation. Structurally, PFAS carboxylates (like PFOA) resemble endogenous fatty acids and can bind to the ligand-binding domain of PPAR$\alpha$.<sup>53</sup> In rodents, strong activation of PPAR$\alpha$ leads to a pleiotropic response known as "peroxisome proliferation," characterized by massive hepatomegaly, enzyme induction, and ultimately liver tumors.

Humans are generally considered refractory to PPAR$\alpha$-mediated peroxisome proliferation, leading to historical debates about the human relevance of rodent liver tumor data.<sup>45</sup> However, PPAR$\alpha$ activation also regulates immune responses, lipid transport, and cell proliferation—pathways that _are_ conserved and relevant to humans. Furthermore, PFASs have been shown to interact with other nuclear receptors, including PPAR$\gamma$, the Constitutive Androstane Receptor (CAR), and the Pregnane X Receptor (PXR), suggesting a multi-receptor mode of action that disrupts broader metabolic homeostasis.<sup>53</sup>

### 4.2. Epigenetic Modifications

The IARC 2025 assessment placed significant weight on evidence of epigenetic alterations. PFASs have been shown to induce global hypomethylation and site-specific hypermethylation in human cells.<sup>55</sup> These changes can alter gene expression without changing the DNA sequence, potentially leading to genomic instability and cancer. For example, exposure to PFASs has been linked to methylation changes in genes like _IGF2_ (insulin-like growth factor 2), which is critical for growth and development. Epigenetic dysregulation may also underlie the long-term effects of in utero exposure, effectively "programming" the fetus for metabolic or developmental disorders later in life—a concept known as the Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD).<sup>56</sup>

### 4.3. Oxidative Stress

PFAS exposure induces the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and depletes antioxidant defenses (e.g., glutathione) in hepatic and immune cells.<sup>55</sup> Oxidative stress is a convergent mechanism that links liver injury, genotoxicity, and placental dysfunction. Recent studies comparing legacy and emerging PFASs found that PFOA and PFOS mixtures induced synergistic oxidative stress in human cell lines, leading to DNA damage and lipid peroxidation.<sup>59</sup>

### 4.4. Key Characteristics of Carcinogens (KCCs)

The IARC Working Group utilized the "Key Characteristics of Carcinogens" framework to evaluate PFOA and PFOS. PFOA was found to exhibit multiple characteristics: it induces oxidative stress, is immunosuppressive, alters cell proliferation, modulates receptor-mediated effects, and induces epigenetic alterations.<sup>8</sup> This mechanistic strength was pivotal in upgrading the classification to Group 1, as it provided the biological plausibility necessary to bridge the gap left by limited human epidemiological cancer data.

## 5. The "GenX" Problem and Emerging Substitutes

As legacy C8 PFASs were phased out under regulatory pressure, manufacturers introduced replacements such as Hexafluoropropylene Oxide Dimer Acid (HFPO-DA), trade-named "GenX," and perfluorobutane sulfonic acid (PFBS). These compounds were designed with shorter carbon chains or ether linkages to facilitate more rapid breakdown or elimination, theoretically reducing bioaccumulation.

### 5.1. The GenX Exposure Scenario

GenX was introduced in 2009 as a processing aid for fluoropolymer manufacturing. It gained notoriety following the discovery of its widespread contamination of the Cape Fear River watershed in North Carolina, the drinking water source for over 250,000 people in Wilmington and surrounding areas.<sup>13</sup> Unlike PFOA, GenX is not removed by standard municipal water treatment (coagulation/filtration) and requires advanced technologies like reverse osmosis or granular activated carbon, though the latter is less effective for short-chain species due to rapid breakthrough.<sup>6</sup>

### 5.2. Comparative Toxicity: GenX vs. PFOA

GenX was marketed as a safer alternative. However, toxicological assessments indicate that the liver remains the primary target organ. Animal studies report hepatocellular necrosis, hypertrophy, and cytoplasmic alteration ("single cell necrosis") following oral exposure.<sup>29</sup>

The EPA's 2021 toxicity assessment for GenX established a chronic Reference Dose (RfD) of $3 \times 10^{-6}$ mg/kg-day. For comparison, the EPA's RfD for PFOA is similarly low ($1.5 \times 10^{-9}$ mg/kg-day is the basis for the interim HA, though final values vary by jurisdiction). This indicates that GenX is a highly potent hepatotoxicant.<sup>29</sup>

Crucially, recent data from the GenX Exposure Study challenges the assumption of rapid human elimination. While GenX has a half-life of hours in rats, preliminary serum analysis from Wilmington residents suggests a much longer half-life in humans, potentially ranging from months to over a year, though precise estimates are still being refined.<sup>28</sup> This suggests that the human OAT transporters may recognize the fluoroether structure more effectively than the rodent homologs, leading to unexpected retention.

### 5.3. Regrettable Substitution

The case of GenX exemplifies "regrettable substitution." While GenX may be slightly less bioaccumulative than PFOA, it is highly mobile in water, more difficult to remediate, and exerts toxicity on the same target organs (liver, immune system, development) via similar mechanisms (PPAR$\alpha$ agonism).<sup>61</sup> Emerging analogs of GenX (e.g., PFDMO2HpA) have shown even greater hepatotoxicity in mice than GenX itself, mediated by PPAR$\alpha$.<sup>60</sup> The EPA’s establishment of a 10 ppt MCL for GenX—an extremely low limit—signals that regulatory agencies view these replacements as significant health hazards, not benign alternatives.<sup>10</sup>

## 6. Regulatory Context

The accumulation of health evidence has precipitated a global regulatory crackdown on PFASs, shifting from advisory guidelines to enforceable standards.

### 6.1. United States: The 2024 National Primary Drinking Water Regulation

In April 2024, the U.S. EPA finalized the first nationwide, legally enforceable drinking water standards for PFASs. This regulation represents a watershed moment in environmental policy.

- **Enforceable MCLs:** PFOA and PFOS are limited to **4.0 ppt**. PFNA, PFHxS, and GenX are limited to **10 ppt**.
- **MCL Goals (MCLGs):** The Goal for PFOA and PFOS is set at **zero**. This reflects the EPA’s determination, aligned with the IARC classification, that PFOA is a likely human carcinogen with no safe threshold for exposure.<sup>10</sup>
- **Hazard Index:** Recognizing that PFASs co-occur, the EPA established a unitless Hazard Index of 1.0 for mixtures of PFHxS, PFNA, HFPO-DA, and PFBS. This approach accounts for the additivity of toxicity, ensuring that water containing multiple PFASs below their individual limits is still regulated if the cumulative risk is high.<sup>10</sup>

### 6.2. European Union: TWI and Universal Restriction

The European approach emphasizes mixture toxicity and class-based restriction.

- **EFSA TWI:** In 2020, EFSA established a group Tolerable Weekly Intake (TWI) of **4.4 ng/kg body weight per week** for the sum of four PFASs (PFOA, PFOS, PFNA, PFHxS). This value is driven by the critical effect of vaccine antibody suppression in children.<sup>4</sup>
- **REACH Restriction:** The European Chemicals Agency (ECHA) is currently evaluating a universal restriction proposal submitted by five member states. Updated in 2025, this proposal aims to ban the manufacture, use, and placing on the market of nearly all PFASs (as a class >10,000 substances) with limited, time-bound derogations for essential uses (e.g., medical devices, protective gear).<sup>64</sup> This represents a fundamental shift from substance-by-substance regulation to a class-based approach, intended to prevent future regrettable substitutions.

## 7. Conclusion

The health impacts of Per- and Polyfluoroalkyl Substances (PFASs) are profound, multifaceted, and pervasive. The scientific consensus has moved decisively from viewing these chemicals as inert industrial surfactants to recognizing them as potent multisystem toxicants. The convergence of "sufficient" animal carcinogenicity data, "strong" mechanistic evidence of epigenetic and immune disruption, and consistent epidemiological associations with low birth weight, dyslipidemia, and reduced vaccine response has justified the recent, stringent regulatory actions by the U.S. EPA and IARC.

The replacement of legacy PFASs with emerging alternatives like GenX has not resolved the public health threat; rather, it has shifted the burden to compounds that are equally persistent in the environment and potentially just as toxic, while being harder to remediate due to their mobility. The unique toxicokinetics of PFASs in humans—specifically their extended half-lives driven by renal reabsorption—render standard animal safety testing challenging and necessitate conservative safety factors to protect human health.

Future research must prioritize the characterization of the thousands of unstudied PFASs, likely through high-throughput in vitro screening and class-based read-across approaches, as evaluating them one by one is infeasible. Additionally, prospective epidemiological studies are needed to definitively quantify cancer risks and the long-term metabolic consequences of in utero exposure. As regulatory frameworks shift towards class-based restrictions, the elimination of non-essential PFAS uses appears to be the only viable strategy to mitigate the accumulating global burden of these forever chemicals.
