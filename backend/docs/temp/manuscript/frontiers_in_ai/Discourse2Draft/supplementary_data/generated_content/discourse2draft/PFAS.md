# Title: Health Impacts of Per- and Polyfluoroalkyl Substances (PFASs): A Comprehensive Review
## I. Abstract
Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) contamination poses growing health risks worldwide. Population studies link higher PFAS levels in the blood to increased overall mortality, cardiovascular disease, cancer, reproductive problems, developmental delays and lower bone density in young people. Laboratory research shows that PFAS mixtures disrupt fat metabolism in liver cells, weaken immune responses—especially in children—and trigger changes in gene expression and DNA integrity that may affect future generations. The heaviest burdens fall on marginalized communities living near polluted sites, who face higher PFAS exposures, limited healthcare access and added mental health stress, yet lack consistent monitoring guidelines. Addressing these challenges calls for advanced human-based testing models, detailed exposure tracking, and long-term studies from birth through adulthood. Standardized methods must assess both long-lasting and newer PFAS chemicals, consider social and environmental stressors, and guide practical, fair policies and cleanup efforts globally.
## II. Introduction & Chemical Background
Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) are defined by the presence of perfluorinated alkyl chains in which each carbon atom is fully or partially substituted with fluorine atoms. This fluorination imparts extraordinary thermal and chemical stability owing to the carbon–fluorine bond, which is among the strongest and shortest in organic chemistry and confers exceptional resistance to environmental degradation  [1]. PFAS molecules typically terminate in polar head groups—most commonly carboxylate (perfluoroalkyl carboxylic acids, PFCAs), sulfonate (perfluoroalkyl sulfonic acids, PFSAs), or sulfonamide (fluoroalkyl sulfonamides, FASAs)—and this structural diversity governs their speciation, partitioning behavior, and analytical detectability in environmental and biological matrices  [2].

Long-chain PFAS are conventionally defined as perfluoroalkyl carboxylates or sulfonates containing seven or more fully fluorinated carbon atoms (e.g., perfluorooctanoic acid, PFOA, and perfluorooctane sulfonic acid, PFOS). Their extended fluorinated tails increase hydrophobic interactions and protein binding affinity, resulting in prolonged biological half-lives, greater potential for bioaccumulation, and well-characterized associations with adverse health outcomes such as renal and hepatic toxicity  [1, 3]. By contrast, short-chain PFAS alternatives, including perfluorohexanoic acid (PFHxA) and emerging perfluoroalkylether carboxylates such as hexafluoropropylene oxide dimer acid (GenX, HFPO-DA), were developed to reduce bioaccumulation potential. Though these shorter homologs exhibit lower bioaccumulative tendencies, their persistence and environmental mobility remain significant concerns  [4, 5].
## III. Exposure Pathways & Toxicokinetics
Drinking water contamination has emerged as a principal route of human PFAS exposure, driven by industrial discharges, landfill leachate, and insufficient removal during wastewater treatment. Nationwide surveys have documented PFAS detection in public water systems supplying over 16 million Americans across 33 states, with measured concentrations frequently exceeding emerging health advisory levels. Contamination often originates upstream of potable water intakes, where PFAS-laden effluents from manufacturing and municipal wastewater plants enter source waters and evade conventional treatment barriers, leading to chronic low-level exposures via tap water consumption  [6, 7].
Food packaging represents another major exposure pathway, as perfluorinated coatings impart oil and water repellency to paper wrappers, cardboard containers, and nonstick cookware. Under typical use conditions, PFAS transfer from packaging into food matrices—especially fatty foods—has been repeatedly demonstrated. Recent analyses of commercially available seafood and prepared foods have detected PFAS in a substantial fraction of samples, underscoring the role of dietary intake from packaged foods as a significant contributor to total body burden  [1, 3].
Indoor dust ingestion disproportionately affects infants and toddlers through frequent hand-to-mouth behaviors and close contact with treated carpets, upholstery, and consumer products. Meta-analyses have shown that dustborne PFAS concentrations correlate strongly with those in residential and occupational environments, leading to elevated daily intakes on a body weight basis for young children relative to adults  [8].
Dermal uptake of PFAS can occur through direct contact with PFAS-treated textiles, firefighting gear, and other pore-blocking coatings. Although transdermal permeability of long-chain PFAS is limited, repeated exposure to contaminated fabrics and dust may cumulatively contribute to internal doses, particularly in occupational settings such as firefighting where side-chain fluorinated polymers degrade and release small volatile PFAS and particulate matter  [9].
Once internalized, the amphiphilic structure of PFAS promotes binding to serum albumin and other plasma proteins, driving bioaccumulation in protein-rich tissues such as liver and blood. Maternal transfer across the placenta and via breast milk further propagates PFAS to the developing fetus and neonate. Differences in chain length and head group chemistry govern tissue distribution and retention times across species  [1, 3].
PFAS exhibit pronounced biomagnification through aquatic and terrestrial food chains, with field studies reporting elevated concentrations in predatory fish, seabird eggs, and mammalian predators far above ambient water levels. The persistent nature of the carbon–fluorine backbone ensures that trophic magnification factors often exceed two- to five-fold per trophic level  [10].
Elimination of PFAS occurs primarily via renal and biliary routes, with fecal excretion playing a secondary role. Long-chain PFCAs and PFSAs exhibit multi-year serum elimination half-lives in humans, whereas their shorter-chain analogs are more readily cleared in weeks to months. In contrast, common laboratory animal models demonstrate half-lives measured in days to weeks, complicating direct extrapolation of toxicokinetic data to human risk assessments  [10].
## IV. System-Specific Health Impacts (The Core Analysis)
PFAS exposure has been consistently linked to perturbations in hepatic function, manifesting as elevations in serum transaminases and histopathological hallmarks of steatosis. In differentiated HepaRG hepatocytes, exposure to individual PFAS compounds and complex mixtures disrupts lipid homeostasis by altering the expression of genes involved in peroxisomal β-oxidation, fatty acid synthesis, and cholesterol efflux, underscoring a direct molecular impact on critical metabolic pathways  [11].

In vivo investigations employing liver-chimeric humanized mice have illuminated human-relevant adverse outcome pathways beyond rodent-specific PPARα activation. Histological analysis revealed mild to moderate steatosis across PFAS-treated groups, with PFOA inducing the most pronounced lipid accumulation. These exposures also perturb circulating lipoprotein fractions and disrupt intrahepatic bile acid homeostasis, collectively evidencing PFAS-driven hepatocellular dysfunction  [12].

Regulatory assessments by the U.S. EPA, IARC, and EFSA converge on a classification of “sufficient” evidence for hepatotoxicity attributable to PFOA and PFOS, reflecting consistent mechanistic, animal, and supported human biomarker data. However, for emerging short-chain analogs and novel replacements, the evidence remains “limited/suggestive,” highlighting the need for further longitudinal and mechanistic studies.
### A. Immunotoxicity
PFAS exposure has been shown to disrupt both innate and adaptive immune processes by altering cytokine secretion patterns and impairing antibody production. Experimental evidence indicates that exposure to persistent analogs such as PFOS shifts cytokine balance away from Th1-type responses—characterized by reduced IL-2 and IFN-γ secretion—and towards Th2-driven profiles, concomitant with diminished T cell–dependent antibody responses (TDAR) in rodent models  [13].

Dose-dependent immunosuppression has been consistently observed in functional assays. For instance, PFOA, PFOS, hexafluoropropylene oxide dimer acid (HFPO-DA), and complex aqueous film-forming foam (AFFF) mixtures elicit significant decreases in serum IgM and IgG titers, with threshold serum concentrations sometimes below 1 μg/mL; these findings underlie regulatory focus on vaccine antibody response as a critical endpoint for risk assessment  [13].

Human epidemiological investigations align with these experimental observations, demonstrating inverse correlations between PFAS body burdens and vaccine‐specific antibody levels in children (e.g., diphtheria, tetanus), as well as associations with heightened incidence of infectious diseases such as lower respiratory tract infections. Prenatal and early‐life PFAS exposures appear particularly detrimental to immune competence, with emerging data also suggesting impaired antibody responses to SARS-CoV-2 antigens among highly exposed groups. These human findings reinforce the derivation of a tolerable weekly intake of 4.4 ng/kg body weight for the combined PFAS group by EFSA  [10, 13].
### B. Carcinogenicity
The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) re-evaluated the carcinogenicity of perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA) and perfluorooctanesulfonic acid (PFOS) in November 2023, ultimately classifying PFOA as “carcinogenic to humans” (Group 1) based on sufficient evidence from epidemiological studies of occupationally and environmentally exposed cohorts  [14]. PFOS, in contrast, was designated as “possibly carcinogenic to humans” (Group 2B), reflecting more limited and inconsistent associations with cancer endpoints in both animal bioassays and human observational research  [14].

At the molecular level, activation of the nuclear receptor peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARα) remains the principal adverse outcome pathway implicated in PFAS-driven hepatocarcinogenesis, as demonstrated in humanized liver mouse models. Chronic PFOA and PFOS exposure induces PPARα-mediated peroxisome proliferation, oxidative stress, and dysregulated lipid metabolism, culminating in hepatocyte hypertrophy and nodular hyperplasia  [12]. Structural studies further reveal that PFOA engages multiple ligand‐binding sites on PPARα, including the activation function‐2 (AF‐2) surface crucial for coactivator recruitment, thereby amplifying transcriptional disruption of genes governing cell proliferation and apoptosis  [15].

Epidemiological investigations of PFAS exposure and cancer risk in human populations have yielded sporadic positive findings, leading IARC to characterize the overall human evidence as inadequate. Cohort studies in highly exposed communities report modest elevations in testicular and kidney cancer incidence, yet these associations have not been consistently replicated across geographically diverse populations, in part due to exposure misclassification and limited statistical power  [14].

Genotoxicity assays indicate that PFAS mixtures can provoke DNA fragmentation and hamper mismatch repair activity in vitro, while oxidative stress assays detect elevated levels of 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) without overt strand breaks, suggesting an indirect mechanism of mutagenesis via reactive oxygen species generation  [15]. Such findings underscore the potential for PFAS to contribute to genomic instability at sub‐cytotoxic concentrations.

Rodent bioassays and humanized liver‐chimeric mouse studies corroborate the hepatocarcinogenic potential of long‐chain PFAS, with PFOA‐treated animals exhibiting dose‐dependent increases in hepatic adenomas and carcinomas. Histopathological analyses reveal marked steatosis, altered bile acid homeostasis, and dysregulated LDL/VLDL profiles that precede tumor formation, aligning with mechanistic evidence of PPARα‐driven dyslipidemia  [11, 12].

Dose–response assessments for PFOA and PFOS tumor endpoints have informed guideline development for drinking water standards. Four of eight U.S. state guideline levels for PFOA derive from developmental toxicities, three from liver toxicity, and one from mammary gland effects, with total uncertainty factors ranging from 30 to 100 to account for interspecies variability and exposure uncertainties  [6].

Bioaccumulation of PFAS in human tissues, notably the liver and serum, amplifies internal dosimetry concerns for carcinogenic risk. Their persistence and ability to partition into protein‐rich compartments underscore the need to consider lifetime exposure and tissue burdens in quantitative cancer risk models  [3].
### C. Developmental & Reproductive Toxicity
PFAS have been detected in maternal, cord, and newborn serum, demonstrating their ability to traverse the placental barrier with estimated transfer efficiencies of 30–79%. In addition to in utero exposure, breast milk contributes substantially to infant PFAS burden, accounting for approximately 83–99% of total daily intake. Infants and toddlers are particularly vulnerable owing to underdeveloped xenobiotic detoxification mechanisms, higher respiration and ingestion rates relative to body weight, and behaviors that increase contact with contaminated dust and surfaces, such as crawling and frequent hand-to-mouth activity  [8].

Epidemiological birth-cohort studies have consistently associated prenatal PFAS exposure with shorter gestational durations and lower birth weights, indicating an elevated risk of preterm delivery. Mechanistically, these exposures are hypothesized to inhibit ten-eleven translocation (TET) enzymes responsible for converting 5-methylcytosine to 5-hydroxymethylcytosine, leading to reduced 5-hmC levels and disrupted epigenetic programming during critical windows of fetal development  [16].

An epigenome-wide association study of placental tissue across five PFAS compounds revealed differential DNA methylation at loci implicated in cardiometabolic regulation, with perfluorohexane sulfonate (PFHxS) producing the most pronounced epigenetic alterations. Variability in analytical methods and exposure assessment intervals across studies has contributed to inconsistent findings, underscoring the necessity for standardized protocols to facilitate robust cross-study comparisons and causal inference  [16].

Beyond epigenetic perturbations, PFAS elicit endocrine-disrupting effects through partial agonism of nuclear receptors. Structural and computational studies indicate that PFOA and related compounds engage multiple ligand-binding pockets, including the AF-2 surface essential for coactivator recruitment, thereby modulating transcriptional networks that govern lipid metabolism, cell proliferation, and hormone synthesis. High-throughput screening data from ToxCast demonstrate receptor activation at low micromolar concentrations for common PFAS such as PFOA, PFOS, PFNA, and PFHxS, highlighting the potential for endocrine disruption at environmentally relevant exposure levels  [15].
### D. Metabolic & Endocrine Disruption
Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) perturb metabolic homeostasis through direct interactions with nuclear receptors central to lipid handling and energy balance. PFAS exhibit partial agonist activity at both PPARα and PPARγ, engaging multiple orthosteric and allosteric sites within the ligand-binding domain, including near the activation function-2 surface critical for coactivator recruitment. These interactions recapitulate aspects of PPARα-mediated hepatic fatty acid oxidation and PPARγ-driven adipogenic lipid storage, with ToxCast assays revealing activation concentrations in the low micromolar range for PFOA, PFOS, PFNA and PFHxS  [15, 17].

In obesity-prone rodent models, selective agonism of PPARα and PPARβ/δ has been shown to induce beige adipocyte formation and restore insulin sensitivity, normalizing oral glucose tolerance tests to control levels. Fenofibrate-treated high-fat diet mice developed UCP1-positive adipocytes expressing thermogenic genes PRDM16 and BMP8b in subcutaneous white adipose tissue, while both PPARα and PPARβ/δ agonists ameliorated glucose intolerance and reduced area under the glucose curve by up to 29%  [18].

Epidemiological analyses consistently link higher serum PFOS concentrations to dyslipidemia, notably inverse associations with total and LDL cholesterol across age and sex groups. Humanized liver-chimeric mice dosed with PFOA, PFOS and GenX recapitulated alterations in LDL/VLDL profiles and bile acid pools, and exhibited mild to moderate hepatic steatosis without overt elevations in standard liver injury markers. In vitro, differentiated HepaRG cells exposed to realistic PFAS mixtures demonstrated modest but reproducible intracellular triglyceride accumulation, highlighting potential mixture effects on hepatocellular lipid regulation  [10-12].

Beyond PPARs, high-throughput screening via the ToxCast program predicts that thousands of PFAS congeners can bind to a spectrum of nuclear receptors—including CAR, FXR, and ERα—underscoring a broader endocrine-disrupting capacity at environmentally relevant concentrations. These data collectively suggest that PFAS-mediated nuclear receptor interference represents a conserved mechanism driving metabolic perturbations that span lipid dysregulation, insulin resistance and adipose tissue remodeling  [15, 17].
## V. Mechanisms of Action
PFAS interactions with peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs) constitute a primary biological plausibility mechanism underpinning lipid dysregulation and metabolic derangements. Structural analyses have revealed that prototypical PFAS such as PFOA engage multiple ligand-binding pockets within PPARα, including an orthosteric site and a secondary pocket near the activation function-2 (AF-2) surface crucial for coactivator recruitment. This allosteric modulation confers partial agonist activity at low micromolar concentrations, thereby subverting physiological transcriptional regulation of genes governing fatty acid β-oxidation, lipid transport, and energy expenditure  [15].

In parallel, PFAS have been shown to perturb redox homeostasis by promoting reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, impairing mitochondrial respiratory chain function, and triggering lipid peroxidation. These events lead to mitochondrial dysfunction and compromise bioenergetic capacity, exacerbating insulin resistance and hepatocellular injury. Although the precise molecular initiators of ROS production remain to be fully elucidated, in vitro studies indicate that PFAS accumulate within mitochondrial membranes, disrupting electron transport and antioxidant defenses.

Emerging evidence further implicates epigenetic alterations as a long-term mechanism of PFAS toxicity. Prenatal exposure to PFAS has been associated with aberrant DNA methylation patterns within promoter regions of genes critical for development and metabolic regulation. These modifications appear to arise through direct interference with DNA methyltransferases and ten-eleven translocation (TET) enzymes, as well as indirect effects via oxidative stress and one-carbon metabolism perturbations. Such epigenomic reprogramming during sensitive developmental windows may confer persistent changes in gene expression, thereby predisposing individuals to metabolic and developmental disorders later in life  [16].
## VI. The "GenX" Problem
GenX (perfluoro-2-methyl-3-oxahexanoic acid) was introduced as a short-chain alternative to PFOA but has since been ubiquitously detected in drinking water and food supplies worldwide, prompting escalating public health concerns. Monitoring data reveal environmental concentrations approaching or exceeding updated U.S. EPA health advisory levels of 0.02 ng/L for PFOS and 0.004 ng/L for PFOA, while dietary intake estimates of 3.96 ng/kg body weight per day correspond to 0.78–1.32 times the established oral reference dose (RfD). Epidemiological analyses further link serum GenX levels to depressive symptoms in adults and associate maternal exposure with an increased risk of depression in offspring  [4, 19, 20].

Rodent reproductive studies demonstrate that maternal GenX exposure at doses as low as 5–10 mg/kg/day impairs testicular development and reduces serum testosterone, with single-cell transcriptomic analysis revealing disruptions in Sertoli and Leydig cell gene networks crucial for spermatogenesis. In parallel, liver-chimeric humanized mice provided 1 mg/L GenX in drinking water exhibit hepatic steatosis, upregulation of PPARα target genes, and early markers of steatohepatitis, underscoring the compound’s hepatotoxic potential  [12, 19].

At the molecular level, GenX functions as a partial agonist at PPARα by engaging both orthosteric and AF-2 coactivator sites, thereby dysregulating fatty acid β-oxidation and lipid transport pathways in a manner akin to legacy long-chain PFAS. Despite shorter environmental half-lives, GenX’s resilience to degradation and demonstrable bioconcentration in vertebrate models call into question its designation as a safer substitute, highlighting the risk of regrettable substitution with short-chain PFAS that retain persistence and toxicity profiles similar to their predecessors  [8, 12].
## VII. Regulatory Context & Knowledge Gaps
In its recent proposal under the Safe Drinking Water Act, the U.S. EPA has recommended maximum contaminant levels (MCLs) of 4 ng/L for PFOA and PFOS, mandating compliance by all large public water systems (serving over 10,000 customers) and an additional 800 smaller systems, yet notably excluded PFAS from the Unregulated Contaminant Monitoring Rule 4 (UCMR4) from 2018 to 2020, raising questions about uniform surveillance and enforcement across jurisdictions  [6]. Concurrently, state‐level guidelines remain heterogeneous: some retain thresholds as high as 70 ng/L, resulting in an estimated 0.4–1 million people exposed to combined PFAS concentrations at or above 70 ng/L, and up to 18–80 million people potentially ingesting lower but still concerning PFAS levels in their drinking water  [21].

The European Union has adopted a precautionary approach through REACH, designating PFOS and PFOA as substances of very high concern (SVHCs) and phasing out their manufacture and use under Annex XVII; forthcoming regulatory amendments aim to extend group‐level restrictions to additional perfluoroalkyl ether (PFE) alternatives  [8].

Despite these regulatory advances, several critical knowledge gaps undermine comprehensive risk management. First, humans are co‐exposed to complex PFAS mixtures rather than isolated congeners; in vitro studies using differentiated HepaRG cells have revealed additive and synergistic disruptions in lipid metabolism upon exposure to PFAS cocktails, yet the mechanistic basis for these mixture effects remains poorly characterized  [11]. Ecotoxicological assessments—such as those evaluating hexafluoropropylene oxide-dimer acid impacts on zebrafish embryogenesis—further underscore the paucity of data on mixture toxicity across developmental stages and biological endpoints  [4].

Second, the long-term health impacts of chronic, low-dose PFAS exposure are insufficiently defined. Epigenetic analyses indicate that in utero exposures to sub-nanomolar PFAS concentrations can provoke sex-specific placental and embryonic alterations, yet epidemiological linkage of these molecular perturbations to adult disease phenotypes is hampered by methodological inconsistencies in exposure assessment and detection limits  [16].

Third, there is an urgent need for prospective epidemiological cohorts that integrate longitudinal PFAS exposure profiles—spanning prenatal periods through adulthood—and encompass mental health outcomes, particularly within marginalized communities disproportionately burdened by environmental contamination  [1, 16].

Finally, regulatory oversight of emerging PFAS alternatives lags behind their widespread adoption. Short-chain fluorinated ethers, promoted as safer substitutes, exhibit environmental persistence and bioaccumulation dynamics akin to legacy PFAS, yet remain largely exempt from current MCL and REACH restrictions, highlighting a gap in precautionary governance  [8]. Moreover, standardized cumulative risk assessment frameworks that account for co‐exposures to multiple PFAS congeners, co‐contaminants, and nonchemical stressors are not yet established, impeding holistic health risk quantification and community engagement strategies  [1, 21].
## VIII. Conclusion
Mounting epidemiological data underscore that PFAS exposure poses significant public health challenges in both highly contaminated regions and general populations. Cohort studies in Italy and elsewhere have demonstrated associations between elevated serum PFAS concentrations and increased all-cause mortality, cardiovascular disease, cancer risk, and reproductive dysfunction, as well as developmental delays and reduced bone mineral density in youth  [14, 22].

Mechanistic investigations reveal that humans are exposed to complex PFAS mixtures that disrupt critical biological pathways. In vitro models using human hepatocyte analogs show additive perturbations of lipid metabolism, while immune cell assays indicate PFAS-induced immunotoxicity—particularly in pediatric populations. Concurrently, emerging neurotoxicological research highlights PFAS-mediated epigenetic and genotoxic alterations that may underlie transgenerational effects  [11, 13, 15].

The burden of PFAS exposure is disproportionately borne by marginalized communities, where environmental injustice compounds health risks. Residents near contaminated sites face elevated body burdens, restricted healthcare access, and heightened vulnerability to mental health stressors linked to chronic chemical exposure, yet clinical monitoring and biomonitoring guidelines remain inconsistent and underutilized  [1].

To address these challenges, future research must integrate advanced human-relevant models and high-resolution exposomic methods to elucidate adverse outcome pathways. Longitudinal birth-to-adulthood cohorts are needed to link prenatal epigenetic programming to adult disease phenotypes, while standardized cumulative risk frameworks should evaluate legacy and emerging PFAS congeners alongside nonchemical stressors. Investigations into the environmental fate, bioaccumulation potential of short-chain substitutes, and pragmatic mitigation strategies in resource-limited settings are equally imperative to inform equitable policy interventions  [8, 12, 16, 23].
