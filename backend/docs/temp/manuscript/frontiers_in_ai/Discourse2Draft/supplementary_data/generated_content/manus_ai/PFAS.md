# Health Impacts of Per- and Polyfluoroalkyl Substances (PFASs): A Comprehensive Review

**Author:** Manus AI, Senior Environmental Epidemiologist and Toxicologist

---

## Abstract

Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFASs) represent a diverse class of persistent organic pollutants ubiquitously detected in the environment and human populations globally [1]. This comprehensive review synthesizes current epidemiological human data and toxicological animal studies to evaluate the health risks associated with legacy PFAS, such as perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA) and perfluorooctane sulfonic acid (PFOS), and emerging replacements, including GenX and perfluorobutane sulfonic acid (PFBS). The primary health concerns center on immunotoxicity, carcinogenicity, developmental, and metabolic disruption. Epidemiological evidence consistently links exposure to elevated serum PFAS concentrations with adverse outcomes, including reduced vaccine response in children, altered lipid metabolism, and increased risk of certain cancers [2] [3]. Toxicological studies provide biological plausibility, primarily through the activation of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPAR-$\alpha$) and induction of oxidative stress [4]. A critical consensus is that while the substitution of long-chain PFAS with shorter-chain alternatives was intended to reduce bioaccumulation, the newer compounds often exhibit similar toxicity profiles, raising concerns about "regrettable substitution." This review underscores the need for a unified regulatory approach and further research into mixture toxicity to address the significant public health implications of these "forever chemicals."

---

## 1. Introduction & Chemical Background

Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFASs) are a large family of synthetic organofluorine compounds characterized by a fully or partially fluorinated carbon chain attached to a functional group [5]. Their widespread use since the 1940s in industrial and consumer products—including non-stick coatings, fire-fighting foams, and stain-resistant fabrics—stems from the exceptional stability imparted by the **carbon-fluorine (C-F) bond**. This bond is one of the strongest in organic chemistry, contributing to the extreme persistence of PFAS in the environment and biological systems, earning them the moniker "forever chemicals" [6].

Chemically, PFASs are often distinguished by the length of their fluorinated carbon chain:

- **Long-chain compounds:** Typically defined as those with eight or more carbons for perfluoroalkyl carboxylates (PFCAs, e.g., PFOA, C8) and six or more carbons for perfluoroalkyl sulfonates (PFSAs, e.g., PFOS, C8) [7]. These compounds generally exhibit greater bioaccumulation potential.
- **Short-chain compounds:** Defined as those with fewer than eight carbons for PFCAs (e.g., perfluorohexanoic acid, PFHxA, C6) and fewer than six carbons for PFSAs (e.g., PFBS, C4) [7]. These were introduced as replacements due to their lower tendency to bioaccumulate, though they are often more mobile in water.

## 2. Exposure Pathways & Toxicokinetics

### 2.1. Exposure Pathways

Human exposure to PFAS is nearly ubiquitous and occurs through multiple primary routes [8]:

1.  **Drinking Water:** Contamination of public and private water supplies, particularly near manufacturing sites, military bases, and fire-training areas, is a major source of exposure [9].
2.  **Food:** Ingestion of contaminated food, including fish and seafood from contaminated waters, and food packaged in materials treated with PFAS (e.g., grease-resistant paper) [10].
3.  **Dust and Indoor Air:** Inhalation and ingestion of house dust containing PFAS shed from consumer products like carpets and upholstery [11].
4.  **Dermal Absorption:** While generally considered limited, some dermal exposure can occur through contact with contaminated water or products [12].

### 2.2. Toxicokinetics

PFAS are readily absorbed and distributed throughout the body, primarily binding to serum proteins. Their toxicokinetics are characterized by significant differences between humans and laboratory animals, particularly concerning elimination half-lives [13].

- **Bioaccumulation and Biomagnification:** Long-chain PFAS, such as PFOA and PFOS, exhibit high bioaccumulation potential in humans and wildlife. They are resistant to metabolic breakdown and are primarily eliminated unchanged through the kidneys. Biomagnification, the increase in concentration at successive trophic levels, has been observed in aquatic and terrestrial food chains [14].
- **Serum Half-Lives:** The most striking toxicokinetic difference is the elimination half-life ($T_{1/2}$). In humans, the estimated serum half-lives for legacy PFAS are exceptionally long, reflecting their persistence [15]:
  - **PFOA:** Estimated human $T_{1/2}$ ranges from approximately 2.1 to 4 years.
  - **PFOS:** Estimated human $T_{1/2}$ ranges from approximately 3.3 to 7 years.
  - **Animal Data Discrepancy:** In contrast, the half-lives in common laboratory animals (e.g., rats, mice) are typically much shorter, often measured in days or weeks [15]. This discrepancy is a critical consideration when extrapolating animal toxicity data to human risk assessment, as it suggests that animals clear the compounds much faster, potentially requiring higher doses to achieve equivalent human body burdens.

## 3. System-Specific Health Impacts (The Core Analysis)

The evaluation of health impacts is based on the weight of evidence from major regulatory and scientific assessments, including the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), and the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA).

| Health Endpoint | Legacy PFAS (PFOA/PFOS) | Evidence Classification (Based on Regulatory Consensus) | Key Findings & Discrepancies |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Immunotoxicity** | PFOA, PFOS | **Sufficient Evidence** (Human) [16] | Strongest human evidence is reduced antibody production following vaccination (e.g., in children) [17]. Considered a highly sensitive endpoint for PFAS toxicity. |
| **Carcinogenicity** | PFOA, PFOS | **Limited/Suggestive Evidence** (Human) [18] | IARC classifies PFOA as **Group 2B** (possibly carcinogenic to humans) and PFOS as **Group 3** (not classifiable as to its carcinogenicity to humans) [19]. Human data links PFOA/PFOS to kidney and testicular cancer. Animal studies show liver, testicular, and mammary gland tumors [20]. |
| **Developmental & Reproductive Toxicity** | PFOA, PFOS | **Limited/Suggestive Evidence** (Human) [21] | Epidemiological studies link exposure to decreased fetal growth (low birth weight), pre-eclampsia, and altered pubertal timing [22]. Animal studies show a wider range of effects, including fetal loss and skeletal variations. |
| **Metabolic & Endocrine Disruption** | PFOA, PFOS | **Sufficient Evidence** (Human) [23] | Consistent association with altered lipid metabolism (increased total and LDL cholesterol) and changes in liver enzyme levels [24]. Evidence also supports links to thyroid disease and potential interference with sex hormones [25]. |

### 3.1. Immunotoxicity

Immunotoxicity is recognized as one of the most sensitive endpoints for PFAS exposure. The most compelling human evidence is the association between elevated serum PFAS levels and a **reduced antibody response to routine childhood vaccines** (e.g., diphtheria and tetanus) [17]. This effect, particularly noted in children, suggests a compromised immune system function, even at relatively low exposure levels.

### 3.2. Carcinogenicity

The evidence for carcinogenicity is strong in animal models, where PFOA and PFOS exposure has been linked to tumors in the liver, testes, and mammary glands [20]. In humans, epidemiological studies, notably from highly exposed communities, have shown associations with **kidney cancer** and **testicular cancer** [18]. The IARC classification reflects the current weight of evidence: PFOA is classified as **Group 2B** (possibly carcinogenic to humans), while PFOS is classified as **Group 3** (not classifiable as to its carcinogenicity to humans) [19].

### 3.3. Developmental & Reproductive Toxicity

PFAS readily cross the placenta and are transferred through breast milk, leading to _in utero_ and early-life exposure [26]. Key findings include an association with **low birth weight** and other markers of decreased fetal growth. Reproductive outcomes linked to PFAS exposure include increased risk of pre-eclampsia and altered fertility parameters, such as reduced semen quality and abnormal menstrual cycles [22].

### 3.4. Metabolic & Endocrine Disruption

PFAS are classified as endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) due to their ability to interfere with hormone systems [25]. The most consistent and robust human finding is the association with **dyslipidemia**, specifically elevated total and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol levels [24]. Furthermore, PFAS exposure is linked to alterations in thyroid hormone homeostasis and liver enzyme levels, such as alanine aminotransferase (ALT), indicating potential hepatotoxicity [24].

## 4. Mechanisms of Action

The biological plausibility of the observed health effects is supported by several proposed mechanisms of action:

- **Peroxisome Proliferator-Activated Receptor Alpha (PPAR-$\alpha$) Activation:** This is a well-established mechanism, particularly for PFOA and PFOS in rodents [4]. PPAR-$\alpha$ is a nuclear receptor that regulates lipid metabolism, cell proliferation, and differentiation. Activation of this receptor is thought to drive the observed hepatotoxicity and metabolic changes in animals, though the relevance of this mechanism is debated in humans, as humans express lower levels of PPAR-$\alpha$ in the liver compared to rodents [27].
- **Oxidative Stress:** Many PFAS compounds, including short-chain alternatives, have been shown to induce **oxidative stress** by increasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) and depleting antioxidant defenses [28]. This mechanism is implicated in immunotoxicity, hepatotoxicity, and genotoxicity.
- **Epigenetics:** Emerging research highlights the role of PFAS in altering **epigenetic mechanisms**, such as DNA methylation, histone modification, and non-coding RNA expression [29]. _In utero_ exposure to PFAS has been shown to alter fetal epigenetics, suggesting a mechanism for long-term, transgenerational health effects [30].

## 5. The "GenX" Problem

The phase-out of PFOA and PFOS led to the introduction of replacement compounds, such as **GenX** (a trade name for the short-chain perfluoroether carboxylic acid, HFPO-DA) and **PFBS** (perfluorobutane sulfonic acid). These replacements were designed to be less bioaccumulative due to their shorter chain lengths.

However, a growing body of evidence suggests that these replacements exhibit similar toxicity profiles to their predecessors, a phenomenon termed **"regrettable substitution"** [31].

- **Toxicity Profile:** Toxicological assessments have found that GenX and PFBS are linked to similar health harms, including effects on the liver, kidney, and immune system [32]. The EPA's own assessments have indicated that GenX is nearly as toxic as PFOA and PFOS, with some studies suggesting it may be even more toxic in certain endpoints [33].
- **Toxicokinetics:** While short-chain PFAS have shorter human half-lives, their increased mobility in water makes them harder to remove from drinking water sources, leading to persistent exposure [34]. The substitution, therefore, did not eliminate the hazard but merely altered the toxicokinetic profile and environmental fate, without a clear reduction in intrinsic toxicity.

## 6. Regulatory Context & Knowledge Gaps

### 6.1. Regulatory Context

Regulatory bodies worldwide are moving to establish enforceable limits for PFAS in drinking water.

- **US EPA:** The EPA has proposed Maximum Contaminant Levels (MCLs) for PFOA and PFOS at **4 parts per trillion (ppt)**, individually [35]. Critically, the EPA has also proposed a **Hazard Index** approach to regulate mixtures of other PFAS (including PFHxS, PFNA, HFPO-DA/GenX, and PFBS), recognizing the cumulative risk posed by co-occurring contaminants [35].
- **EU REACH:** The European Union's REACH regulation is moving toward broad restrictions on the manufacture, placing on the market, and use of a wide range of PFASs, aiming for a near-total ban on the entire class of chemicals [36].

### 6.2. Knowledge Gaps

Despite significant research, critical knowledge gaps remain that impede comprehensive risk assessment:

- **Mixture Toxicity:** The vast majority of human exposure involves complex mixtures of dozens of PFAS compounds, yet most toxicological studies focus on single compounds [37]. Understanding the synergistic or additive effects of these mixtures is paramount, a challenge the EPA's new framework attempts to address.
- **Toxicity of Novel and Unknown PFAS:** Thousands of PFAS exist, and the toxicity data for the vast majority of these, especially novel replacements and precursors, are lacking [38].
- **Human Relevance of PPAR-$\alpha$ Activation:** Further clarification is needed on the exact role of PPAR-$\alpha$ in human PFAS toxicity to improve the extrapolation of rodent data.

---

## 7. Conclusion

The weight of evidence from both epidemiological and toxicological studies confirms that exposure to PFASs, including both legacy and emerging compounds, poses a significant public health risk. The most robust human evidence points to immunotoxicity (reduced vaccine response), metabolic disruption (dyslipidemia), and associations with kidney and testicular cancer. The stark difference in serum half-lives between humans and animals necessitates caution in relying solely on animal toxicity data for human risk assessment. The emergence of replacements like GenX and PFBS, which demonstrate similar intrinsic toxicity, highlights the failure of a compound-by-compound regulatory approach and the urgent need to regulate PFAS as a class. Future research must prioritize the assessment of mixture toxicity and the health effects of the thousands of understudied PFAS to inform effective public health interventions and regulatory policy.
